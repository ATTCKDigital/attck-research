# Evidence review: Emotion and First Impressions in UI

Article: [Emotion and First Impressions in UI](https://attck.com/blog/emotion-and-first-impressions-in-ui/)  
Reviewed: November 2026 · Method version 2 · [How these reviews work](https://github.com/ATTCKDigital/attck-research/blob/main/.claude/skills/evidence-review/METHOD.md)

## How this review works

Each factual claim in the article was checked against the source it cites, then challenged by reviewers whose job was to find the strongest evidence against it: replication attempts, meta-analyses, later papers that cite the original work, and research from other fields that study the same mechanism. A separate reviewer weighed both sides, and a script re-checked every quoted source. Where a claim was overstated or misattributed, the article was corrected. The research below supports the article as it now reads.

## Background research

### Introduction

**In 2006 a Carleton University team led by Gitte Lindgaard flashed web home pages for 50 milliseconds and had people rate visual appeal; those ratings were consistent with ratings made when people could look ten times longer (500 ms).**

The consistency in Lindgaard et al. is a correlation between page-level average ratings at 50 ms and 500 ms. Later five-second-test research finds first-impression feedback varies with page complexity and with the viewer.

- [Lindgaard et al., Attention web designers, Behaviour & Information Technology (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1080/01449290500330448): “Throughout, visual appeal ratings were highly correlated from one phase to the next as were the correlations between the 50 ms and 500 ms conditions.”
- [Willis & Todorov, First impressions: Making up your mind after a 100-ms exposure to a face, Psychological Science 17(7), 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01750.x): “Judgments made after a 100-ms exposure correlated highly with judgments made in the absence of time constraints”
- [Ambady & Rosenthal, Thin slices of expressive behavior as predictors of interpersonal consequences: A meta-analysis, Psychological Bulletin 111(2), 1992](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.111.2.256): “Studies using longer periods of behavioral observation did not yield greater predictive accuracy”

**A first impression doesn't get set aside once people start reading. Studies of home pages find it carries into how usable and how trustworthy people judge a site to be, at least until they have used it for a while.**

Lindgaard et al. (2011) and Reinecke et al. (2013) link quick visual appeal to later usability and trust judgments; Sonderegger et al. (2012) found the aesthetic boost to perceived usability waned over two weeks of use.

- [Lindgaard et al., An exploration of relations between visual appeal, trustworthiness and perceived usability of homepages, ACM TOCHI 18(1), 2011](https://doi.org/10.1145/1959022.1959023): “The results suggest that all three types of judgments are largely driven by visual appeal, but that cognitively demanding judgments are processed in a qualitatively different manner than visual appeal”
- [Reinecke et al., Predicting users' first impressions of website aesthetics with a quantification of perceived visual complexity and colorfulness, CHI 2013](https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2481281): “This first impression is influential enough to later affect their opinions of a site's usability and trustworthiness.”
- [Thielsch, Engel & Hirschfeld, Expected usability is not a valid indicator of experienced usability, PeerJ Computer Science 1:e19, 2015](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj-cs.19): “The results at both the group and individual level show that expected usability ratings are not significantly related either to experienced usability or objective usability measures.”

### The Feeling Arrives First

**Before people understand a screen, they react to it. The reaction is fast and emotional: clean or cluttered, credible or cheap, made for someone like me or for someone else. It often forms before people have looked closely at features, copy or price.**

The idea that feeling can come before thinking traces to Zajonc (1980) and was debated by Lazarus and others; the web evidence shows fast, stable appeal judgments, not that they precede all conscious thought.

- [Lindgaard et al., Attention web designers, Behaviour & Information Technology (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1080/01449290500330448): “Throughout, visual appeal ratings were highly correlated from one phase to the next as were the correlations between the 50 ms and 500 ms conditions.”
- [Willis & Todorov, First impressions: Making up your mind after a 100-ms exposure to a face, Psychological Science 17(7), 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01750.x): “Judgments made after a 100-ms exposure correlated highly with judgments made in the absence of time constraints”
- [Lindgaard et al., An exploration of relations between visual appeal, trustworthiness and perceived usability of homepages, ACM TOCHI 18(1), 2011](https://doi.org/10.1145/1959022.1959023): “The results suggest that all three types of judgments are largely driven by visual appeal, but that cognitively demanding judgments are processed in a qualitatively different manner than visual appeal”

**On the open web that reaction does real work. Leaving costs one click, as we saw in the first article in this series, so people screen pages fast, and how a page looks is a large part of that screen.**

Liu, White and Dumais (2010) describe an initial screening stage before pages are read in detail; Fogg et al. (2003) found design look was the most mentioned credibility cue (46.1% of comments).

- [Liu, White & Dumais, Understanding web browsing behaviors through Weibull analysis of dwell time, SIGIR 2010](https://doi.org/10.1145/1835449.1835513): “some initial screening has to be passed before a page is examined in detail”
- [Fogg et al., How do users evaluate the credibility of Web sites? DUX 2003](https://doi.org/10.1145/997078.997097): “of the site was mentioned most frequently, being present in 46.1% of the comments.”

**For the many visitors who leave after a quick look, it is the whole experience.**

Dwell-time studies show many page visits end early after an initial screen; the article does not claim a specific percentage.

**Before use, perceived beauty and perceived usability were strongly linked.**

Hassenzahl and Monk (2010) reviewed 25 correlations of beauty with usability and found high variability across studies.

- [Tractinsky, Katz & Ikar, What is beautiful is usable, Interacting with Computers (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0953-5438(00)00031-X): “Pre-experimental measures indicate strong correlations between system's perceived aesthetics and perceived usability.”
- [Statistically controlling for processing fluency reduces the aesthetic-usability effect, CHI EA 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3544549.3585739): “The aesthetic-usability effect was replicated by our data: aesthetics and usability correlated .79.”

**After use, the attractive versions were still rated as more usable, while the systems' actual usability had no comparable effect on those ratings.**

This was one study of a simulated ATM. Sonderegger and Sauer (2010) found a similar effect with phones; Hassenzahl (2004) and a 2014 phone study found usability problems, not looks, drove perceived usability after use.

- [Sonderegger & Sauer, The influence of design aesthetics in usability testing, Applied Ergonomics 41(3), 2010 (Europe PMC record)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19892317): “participants using the highly appealing phone rated their appliance as being more usable than participants operating the unappealing model.”

**Later research has complicated that result. Some studies found the effect fades with repeated use, and others found the reverse, with a system that works well coming to look more attractive. Don Norman still gave the broader idea a lasting name, grounded in research showing that good feelings make people more tolerant of small difficulties.**

Norman's 2002 essay bases 'attractive things work better' on positive-affect research (Isen; Ashby et al.); his 2004 book Emotional Design discusses the ATM studies (recall, not verified).

- [Norman, Emotion & Design: Attractive Things Work Better, Interactions (2002), author's reprint](https://jnd.org/emotion-design-attractive-things-work-better/): “Positive affect makes people more tolerant of minor difficulties and more flexible and creative in finding solutions.”

### What the Eye Judges in 50 Milliseconds

**Both visual complexity and prototypicality shaped beauty ratings within the first 50 ms, and sites that were simple and looked like their category were rated most appealing.**

Reinecke and Gajos (2014) found the complexity level at which appeal peaks differs across groups and countries, and some studies find an inverted-U, where very plain designs also lose appeal.

- [Tuch et al., The role of visual complexity and prototypicality, IJHCS (2012), Google Research publication page](https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/): “Overall, websites with low VC and high PT were perceived as highly appealing.”
- [Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman, Processing fluency and aesthetic pleasure, Personality and Social Psychology Review 8(4), 2004](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327957pspr0804_3): “The more fluently perceivers can process an object, the more positive their aesthetic response.”
- [Winkielman, Halberstadt, Fazendeiro & Catty, Prototypes are attractive because they are easy on the mind, Psychological Science 17(9), 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01785.x): “Critically, fluency mediated the effect of prototypicality on attractiveness, although some effect of prototypicality remained when fluency was controlled.”

**Google's write-up of the work adds the part designers most often miss: the two factors work together. A familiar layout does not rescue a cluttered page, and a clean page built on an unfamiliar structure still pays a price.**

Hekkert, Snelders and van Wieringen (2003) found people prefer novel product designs as long as the novelty does not cost typicality; Pieters, Wedel and Batra (2010) found cluttered ads hurt liking while elaborate design complexity helped.

- [Bargas-Avila, Users love simple and familiar designs, Google Research blog (2012)](https://research.google/blog/users-love-simple-and-familiar-designs-why-websites-need-to-make-a-great-first-impression/): “if the visual complexity of a website is high, users perceive it as less beautiful, even if the design is familiar.”
- [Hekkert, Snelders & van Wieringen, 'Most advanced, yet acceptable': Typicality and novelty as joint predictors of aesthetic preference in industrial design, British Journal of Psychology 94(1), 2003](https://doi.org/10.1348/000712603762842147): “people prefer novel designs as long as the novelty does not affect typicality”

**Their first-impression system is reacting to complexity and unfamiliarity, and research on attractiveness and perceived usability suggests that reaction can color how usable the product seems, at least until people have spent real time with it.**

Thielsch et al. (2015) found usability expected from brief screenshots was unrelated to experienced or objective usability; a 2020 replication found visual clarity links aesthetics and perceived usability.

- [Thielsch, Engel & Hirschfeld, Expected usability is not a valid indicator of experienced usability, PeerJ Computer Science 1:e19, 2015](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj-cs.19): “The results at both the group and individual level show that expected usability ratings are not significantly related either to experienced usability or objective usability measures.”
- [Visual clarity as mediator between usability and aesthetics, Mensch und Computer 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3404983.3409990): “our results add empirical support for the thesis that the connection between aesthetical impression and perceived usability is caused by visual clarity.”

### The Feeling Sets the Terms

**A first impression is not a gate users pass through and forget. It shapes what follows: how easy the product seems, how far people trust it, and quite possibly how patient they are when something goes wrong. That pull is strongest early and can fade as people get to know the product.**

Trust and usability links: Lindgaard et al. (2011), Reinecke et al. (2013). Tolerance: Norman (2002), theory based on positive-affect research. Fading with use: Sonderegger et al. (2012).

- [Lindgaard et al., An exploration of relations between visual appeal, trustworthiness and perceived usability of homepages, ACM TOCHI 18(1), 2011](https://doi.org/10.1145/1959022.1959023): “The results suggest that all three types of judgments are largely driven by visual appeal, but that cognitively demanding judgments are processed in a qualitatively different manner than visual appeal”
- [Reinecke et al., Predicting users' first impressions of website aesthetics with a quantification of perceived visual complexity and colorfulness, CHI 2013](https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2481281): “This first impression is influential enough to later affect their opinions of a site's usability and trustworthiness.”
- [Norman, Emotion & Design: Attractive Things Work Better, Interactions (2002), author's reprint](https://jnd.org/emotion-design-attractive-things-work-better/): “Positive affect makes people more tolerant of minor difficulties and more flexible and creative in finding solutions.”

## What changed in the article

These edits were made on November 12, 2026 after the review.

### Introduction: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** A first impression is not set aside once people start reading; it becomes the lens through which they read the rest of the page.

**Now reads:** A first impression doesn't get set aside once people start reading. Studies of home pages find it carries into how usable and how trustworthy people judge a site to be, at least until they have used it for a while.

**Why:** Studies of home pages do find that a fast visual impression carries into later judgments of usability and trust, so the idea is sound. But the article states it as a general law about reading, with no source, and longer studies show the effect fades as people use a product and learn how well it actually works. Now cites [Lindgaard et al., An exploration of relations between visual appeal, trustworthiness and perceived usability of homepages, ACM TOCHI (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1959022.1959023).

### The Feeling Arrives First: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Before people understand a screen they react to it emotionally (clean or cluttered, credible or cheap), and this happens before any conscious evaluation of features, copy or price.

**Now reads:** Before people understand a screen, they react to it. The reaction is fast and emotional: clean or cluttered, credible or cheap, made for someone like me or for someone else. It often forms before people have looked closely at features, copy or price.

**Why:** Fast evaluative reactions are well documented: people rate pages after 50 ms and faces after 100 ms, and those ratings match slower ones. 'Before any conscious evaluation' is stronger than the evidence, and whether feeling always comes before thinking has been argued over in psychology for decades. The claim is also uncited. Now cites [Zajonc, Feeling and thinking: Preferences need no inferences, American Psychologist (1980)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.35.2.151).

### The Feeling Arrives First: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Because leaving costs one click, a quick emotional read is how people decide whether a page deserves their attention at all.

**Now reads:** On the open web that reaction does real work. Leaving costs one click, as we saw in the first article in this series, so people screen pages fast, and how a page looks is a large part of that screen.

**Why:** Dwell-time research shows people screen web pages quickly and abandon many early, and credibility research shows the look of a site is what people mention most. Nothing shows the screen is mainly emotional rather than a quick check for relevance, so 'emotional read' overstates what is known. Now cites [Liu, White & Dumais, Understanding web browsing behaviors through Weibull analysis of dwell time, SIGIR (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1835449.1835513).

### The Feeling Arrives First: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** For a large share of visitors, the first screen is the whole experience.

**Now reads:** For the many visitors who leave after a quick look, it is the whole experience.

**Why:** It is well established that many web visits end after a short first look, so the claim points the right way. But 'a large share' sounds like a measured figure, and the article gives none and cites nothing. Now cites [Liu, White & Dumais, Understanding web browsing behaviors through Weibull analysis of dwell time, SIGIR (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1835449.1835513).

### The Feeling Arrives First: contested

**Claim reviewed:** Researchers have since debated how far the causal claim reaches, but the direction of the effect has held up well.

**Now reads:** Later research has complicated that result. Some studies replicated it, others found the effect fades with repeated use or does not appear at all, and several found the reverse, with a system that works well coming to look more attractive. Don Norman still gave the broader idea a lasting name, grounded in research showing that good feelings make people more tolerant of small difficulties.

**Why:** The article says the direction of the effect has held up well, but the record since 2000 is split. Some experiments reproduce it, others find no effect of looks on usability ratings at all, including a seven-week study with 110 people, and several find the opposite, with a system that works well being rated more attractive afterward. Meta-analyses do show attractive interfaces modestly help task performance, which is a different claim from looks driving usability ratings. Now cites [Hassenzahl & Monk, The inference of perceived usability from beauty, Human-Computer Interaction (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1080/07370024.2010.500139).

### The Feeling Arrives First: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Don Norman named the effect 'Attractive things work better' because the Tractinsky direction held up.

**Now reads:** Later research has complicated that result. Some studies found the effect fades with repeated use, and others found the reverse, with a system that works well coming to look more attractive. Don Norman still gave the broader idea a lasting name, grounded in research showing that good feelings make people more tolerant of small difficulties.

**Why:** Norman did write 'attractive things work better' in this 2002 essay. But the essay grounds the phrase in research on positive emotion and tolerance of difficulty, not in Tractinsky's study, and its reading list does not cite Tractinsky. So the article's link ('held up well enough that Norman gave it a name') is not in the source.

### What the Eye Judges in 50 Milliseconds: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** A 2012 study by Tuch, Bargas-Avila and colleagues at the University of Basel, run with Google, showed participants screenshots of 119 real websites, some for as little as 17 ms, and asked them to rate how beautiful each looked.

**Now reads:** Participants saw screenshots of 119 real websites for as little as 50 milliseconds, and in a follow-up study for as little as 17 milliseconds, and rated how beautiful each one looked.

**Why:** The details are real but come from two different studies in the paper. The 119 websites were shown for 50 ms and longer in the first study; the 17 ms exposures were in a second study. Participants rated perceived aesthetics, which 'beautiful' renders fairly.

### What the Eye Judges in 50 Milliseconds: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** The two factors interact: a familiar layout does not rescue a cluttered page, and a clean page with an unfamiliar structure still pays a price; simple and familiar together is what reads as beautiful.

**Now reads:** Google's write-up of the work adds the part designers most often miss: the two factors work together. A familiar layout does not rescue a cluttered page, and a clean page built on an unfamiliar structure still pays a price.

**Why:** Google's write-up says both halves: high complexity hurts even when the design is familiar, and unfamiliar simple designs also lose out. 'Interact' suggests a statistical interaction that was not checked in the paper. Work in industrial design adds that novelty can help as long as it does not cost typicality, which is close to the article's own advice.

### What the Eye Judges in 50 Milliseconds: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** A redesign that is more capable on paper can underperform because people's first-impression reaction to complexity and unfamiliarity then colors how usable the product seems, as Tractinsky's work suggests.

**Now reads:** Their first-impression system is reacting to complexity and unfamiliarity, and research on attractiveness and perceived usability suggests that reaction can color how usable the product seems, at least until people have spent real time with it.

**Why:** The article hedges ('suggests'), and there is real support: usability judged from a glance tracks looks rather than actual usability. But Tractinsky studied attractiveness on an ATM simulation, not complexity, unfamiliarity or redesigns, and later studies find the effect fades with use or is explained by how easy the screen is to process. Now cites [Thielsch, Engel & Hirschfeld, Expected usability is not a valid indicator of experienced usability, PeerJ Computer Science (2015)](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj-cs.19).

### Designing the First Glance (Avoid list): contested

**Claim reviewed:** A usability fix should not be expected to change how people feel about a screen that looks cluttered.

**Now reads:** Counting on a usability fix alone to repair a first impression that the visual design is creating.

**Why:** The advice implies that fixing usability will not change how people feel about how a screen looks. One study found beauty ratings stayed put after use, and the visual-clarity work suggests both judgments spring from the same layout qualities, so a fix that leaves the clutter alone may not move them. But several experiments found the reverse: people who used a shop or a phone that worked badly rated it less beautiful afterward, and social psychology finds the same pattern with people, where learning that someone is pleasant raises their attractiveness ratings.

### The Feeling Sets the Terms: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** A first impression sets the terms for what follows: how easy the product seems, how far people trust it, and how patient they are when something goes wrong.

**Now reads:** A first impression is not a gate users pass through and forget. It shapes what follows: how easy the product seems, how far people trust it, and quite possibly how patient they are when something goes wrong. That pull is strongest early and can fade as people get to know the product.

**Why:** Ease and trust are backed by studies of home pages that link quick visual appeal to later usability and trust ratings. Patience when something goes wrong rests on Norman's argument about positive emotion, which is theory more than a tested web finding. The conclusion also leaves out that these effects weaken as people use a product. Now cites [Lindgaard et al., An exploration of relations between visual appeal, trustworthiness and perceived usability of homepages, ACM TOCHI (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1959022.1959023).
