# Evidence review: Reading Research Without Getting Lost

Article: [Reading Research Without Getting Lost](https://attck.com/blog/reading-research-without-getting-lost/)  
Reviewed: November 2026 · Method version 2 · [How these reviews work](https://github.com/ATTCKDigital/attck-research/blob/main/.claude/skills/evidence-review/METHOD.md)

## How this review works

Each factual claim in the article was checked against the source it cites, then challenged by reviewers whose job was to find the strongest evidence against it: replication attempts, meta-analyses, later papers that cite the original work, and research from other fields that study the same mechanism. A separate reviewer weighed both sides, and a script re-checked every quoted source. Where a claim was overstated or misattributed, the article was corrected. The research below supports the article as it now reads.

## Background research

### Two Large Attempts to Check the Record

**In 2015, the Open Science Collaboration published replications of 100 studies from three psychology journals, using high-powered designs and the original materials where available.**

Confirmed against the abstract of the Open Science Collaboration paper.

- [Open Science Collaboration, Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science, Science (2015) (source](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aac4716): “We conducted replications of 100 experimental and correlational studies published in three psychology journals using high-powered designs and original materials when available.”

**97% of the original studies had statistically significant results, 36% of the replications did, and replication effects were on average half the size of the originals.**

The figures match the Open Science Collaboration abstract. A published critique (Gilbert et al., 2016) disputes what the figures mean, not the figures themselves; later large replication projects found similar or larger declines.

- [Klein et al., Many Labs 2, AMPPS (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1177/2515245918810225): “The median comparable Cohen’s ds were 0.60 for the original findings and 0.15 for the replications.”

**Camerer and colleagues replicated 21 social science experiments published in Nature and Science between 2010 and 2015, with samples on average about five times larger than the originals.**

Confirmed against the Camerer et al. (2018) abstract.

- [Camerer et al., Evaluating the Replicability of Social Science Experiments in Nature and Science, Nature Human Behaviour (2018) (source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31346273): “The replications are high powered, with sample sizes on average about five times higher than in the original studies.”

**Thirteen of the 21 replications (62%) found a significant effect in the same direction, and effect sizes again came out at about half.**

Confirmed against the Camerer et al. (2018) abstract.

**Prediction markets put the replication rate at 63.4% and surveys at 60.6%, against an observed 61.9%; researchers' beliefs were strongly related to which studies succeeded, and the authors concluded the failures were not the result of chance alone.**

The prediction-market and survey figures were confirmed in the authors' preprint (SocArXiv), since the published full text is paywalled. Later forecasting projects find markets predict replication with roughly 70% accuracy, useful but not perfect.

- [Camerer et al. (2018), author preprint on SocArXiv (source; publisher full text is paywalled)](https://osf.io/4hmb6/download): “The average prediction market belief of replicating after Stage 2 is a replication rate of 63.4% and the average survey belief is 60.6%, both close to the observed replication rate of 61.9%”

**The 2015 team's correlational tests suggested that the strength of the original evidence predicted success better than who ran either study.**

The Open Science Collaboration reported this as what correlational tests 'suggest'; the sentence now carries that hedge.

**A multi-lab project led by Richard Klein tested 13 effects and found that replicability seemed to depend more on the effect itself than on whether the sample was online or in a lab, in the US or elsewhere.**

Many Labs 1 drew this conclusion from 13 effects and called it a small sample; the larger Many Labs 2 (28 effects) found limited variation across samples and settings, consistent with it.

- [Klein et al., Investigating Variation in Replicability (Many Labs 1), Social Psychology (2014) (source)](https://doi.org/10.1027/1864-9335/a000178): “The results of this small sample of effects suggest that replicability is more dependent on the effect itself than on the sample and setting used to investigate the effect.”

### Two Large Attempts to Check the Record / Where the Extra Size Comes From

**So the two projects disagree on the rate and agree on what matters for a reader. Published effects tend to shrink when someone checks, by about half in these two projects and by more in some larger efforts since, and failures are not random.**

Later replication projects found larger declines than half: Many Labs 2 (median d from 0.60 to 0.15) and SCORE across the social and behavioral sciences (median r from 0.25 to 0.10). Some decline is expected because only significant originals were re-tested.

- [Open Science Collaboration, Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science, Science (2015) (source](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aac4716): “Replication effects were half the magnitude of original effects, representing a substantial decline.”
- [Camerer et al., Nature Human Behaviour (2018), via Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31346273): “The relative effect size of true positives is estimated to be 71%, suggesting that both false positives and inflated effect sizes of true positives contribute to imperfect reproducibility.”

### Where the Extra Size Comes From

**Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn (2011) showed that ordinary flexibility in collecting, analysing and reporting data dramatically raises the real rate of false positives; in many cases a researcher is more likely to find false evidence of an effect than to correctly find none.**

Confirmed against the Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn abstract; later simulation work agrees.

- [Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn, False-Positive Psychology, Psychological Science (2011) (source)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611417632): “flexibility in data collection, analysis, and reporting dramatically increases actual false-positive rates. In many cases, a researcher is more likely to falsely find evidence that an effect exists than to correctly find evidence that it does not.”
- [Frontiers in Psychology (2026)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1769309): “Data-peeking, the practice of stopping data collection once significant results are obtained, poses a significant threat to the credibility of research by increasing the likelihood of observing and publishing false positive results.”

**Preregistration (stating the question and analysis plan before seeing the outcome) keeps a prediction distinct from a story fitted afterwards, a mistake hindsight makes easy.**

Preregistration's value depends on adherence: audits find most preregistered studies deviate from their plans, often without saying so. The article's advice to compare plan and paper addresses this.

- [Nosek et al., The Preregistration Revolution, PNAS (2018) (source)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1708274114): “However, ordinary biases in human reasoning, such as hindsight bias, make it hard to avoid this mistake. An effective solution is to define the research questions and analysis plan before observing the research outcomes-a process called preregistration.”
- [Kaplan & Irvin, PLOS ONE (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0132382): “Pre-registration in clinical trials.gov was strongly associated with the trend toward null findings.”

**Scheel, Schijen and Lakens compared 71 registered reports with 152 standard psychology papers; the first hypothesis was supported in 96% of standard papers and 44% of registered reports.**

- [Scheel, Schijen & Lakens, AMPPS (2021) (source)](https://doi.org/10.1177/25152459211007467): “Analyzing the first hypothesis of each article, we found 96% positive results in standard reports but only 44% positive results in RRs.”
- [Allen & Mehler, PLOS Biology (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3000246): “null findings are substantially more likely to be published via open registered reports in comparison to more conventional methods.”

**Checking takes a little care. A preregistered study usually says so in its methods section and links to the plan on a public registry, and services for this now exist across all disciplines. Compare the plan with the paper, because departures are common and often go unmentioned. If the headline result comes from an analysis the plan never mentioned, read it as a promising lead rather than a confirmed finding.**

Audits of preregistered studies found that 25 of 27 early ones in Psychological Science deviated from their plans, and 72% of a sample in organizational psychology did, 68% with at least one undisclosed change. Comparing plan and paper is still the right habit; it just takes more than a glance.

### The classic that averaged out

**Scheibehenne and colleagues (2010) pooled 63 conditions from 50 experiments, about 5,000 people, and found a mean effect of virtually zero with wide variation between studies.**

- [Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd, Journal of Consumer Research (2010) (source)](https://doi.org/10.1086/651235): “In a meta-analysis of 63 conditions from 50 published and unpublished experiments (N=5,036), we found a mean effect size of virtually zero but considerable variance between studies.”
- [McShane & Böckenholt, Psychometrika (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11336-017-9571-z): “it shows that choice overload varies substantially as a function of the six dependent measures and four moderators examined in the domain”

**Chernev and colleagues' 2015 meta-analysis of 99 observations found choice overload is real under specific conditions: complex choice sets, difficult tasks, uncertain preferences and a goal of minimising effort.**

Confirmed against the source abstract. The paper appeared online in 2014 and in print in 2015.

- [Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman, Journal of Consumer Psychology (2015) (source)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002): “we identify four key factors—choice set complexity, decision task difficulty, preference uncertainty, and decision goal—that moderate the impact of assortment size on choice overload.”

### The number with no denominator

**At Microsoft, a 'too good to be true' alert on a surprising lift usually signals a bug.**

- [Kohavi & Thomke, The Surprising Power of Online Experiments, Harvard Business Review (2017) (source)](https://hbr.org/2017/09/the-surprising-power-of-online-experiments): “triggering a “too good to be true” alert. Usually, such alerts signal a bug, but not in this case.”

### The statistic that doesn't add up

**Nuijten and colleagues checked more than 250,000 p-values from eight psychology journals (1985-2013); half of papers reporting significance tests had at least one inconsistent p-value, and one in eight had an inconsistency that may have changed the statistical conclusion.**

- [Nuijten et al., Behavior Research Methods (2016) (source)](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-015-0664-2): “half of all published psychology papers that use NHST contained at least one p-value that was inconsistent with its test statistic and degrees of freedom. One in eight papers contained a grossly inconsistent p-value”
- [Nuijten et al., PsyArXiv (2017)](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/tcxaj): “we demonstrate that inconsistencies associated with statistical corrections are not what is causing the high estimates of the prevalence of statistical reporting inconsistencies in psychology.”

### Tool: statcheck

**The tool behind that study, described by its authors as a spellchecker for statistics. It finds significance tests reported in APA style, recalculates each p-value and flags the ones that don't match. It usually can't read statistics in tables, so a clean result covers only the tests it found. Run a paper through the web version before your team builds on it.**

The 'spellchecker' description comes from statcheck.io, the authors' web version. Its FAQ notes it usually misses statistics reported in tables.

- [statcheck.io (web version by the statcheck authors)](https://statcheck.io): “statcheck is a "spellchecker" for statistics.”
- [Nuijten et al., PsyArXiv (2017)](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/tcxaj): “We found that statcheck’s sensitivity (true positive rate) and specificity (true negative rate) were high: between 85.3% and 100%, and between 96.0% and 100%, respectively”

## What changed in the article

These edits were made on November 12, 2026 after the review.

### Two Large Attempts to Check the Record: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** The 2015 team found that the strength of the original evidence predicted replication success better than who ran either study.

**Now reads:** The 2015 team's correlational tests suggested that the strength of the original evidence predicted success better than who ran either study.

**Why:** The cited paper says this, but hedged: its correlational tests 'suggest' original evidence strength predicted success better than team characteristics. The article states it as a firm finding. A one-word change restores the paper's caution.

### Two Large Attempts to Check the Record: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** A multi-lab project led by Richard Klein found that replicability depended more on the effect itself than on whether the sample was online or in a lab, in the US or elsewhere.

**Now reads:** A multi-lab project led by Richard Klein tested 13 effects and found that replicability seemed to depend more on the effect itself than on whether the sample was online or in a lab, in the US or elsewhere.

**Why:** The cited paper reaches this conclusion but calls its basis 'this small sample of effects' (13 effects). Many Labs 2, a larger follow-up, found broadly the same pattern, so the claim holds; the article should just signal the small base.

### Two Large Attempts to Check the Record / Where the Extra Size Comes From: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Published effects tend to shrink by about half when someone checks, and failures are not random.

**Now reads:** So the two projects disagree on the rate and agree on what matters for a reader. Published effects tend to shrink when someone checks, by about half in these two projects and by more in some larger efforts since, and failures are not random.

**Why:** Both cited projects found effects roughly halved, and Camerer et al. support 'not random'. But the article turns two projects into a general rule, and larger later efforts found bigger drops: Many Labs 2's median effect fell from d = 0.60 to 0.15 and SCORE's from r = 0.25 to 0.10. 'About half' is closer to a floor than a typical figure, which makes the article's advice to plan for smaller effects stronger, not weaker. Now cites [SCORE collaboration, Nature (2026), via Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/41922700).

### Where the Extra Size Comes From: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** If the headline result comes from an analysis the preregistered plan never mentioned, read it as a promising lead rather than a confirmed finding; checking takes a few minutes.

**Now reads:** Checking takes a little care. A preregistered study usually says so in its methods section and links to the plan on a public registry, and services for this now exist across all disciplines. Compare the plan with the paper, because departures are common and often go unmentioned. If the headline result comes from an analysis the plan never mentioned, read it as a promising lead rather than a confirmed finding.

**Why:** Treating unplanned analyses as leads, not confirmed findings, follows directly from the cited paper. But 'checking takes a few minutes' undersells the job: audits find most preregistered studies depart from their plans and many don't say so, so a reader has to compare the two documents with some care rather than trust the label. Now cites [Preregistration adherence in industrial and organizational psychology, Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1111/joop.70057).

### The classic that averaged out: misattributed

**Claim reviewed:** The jam study is the field's favourite evidence that more choice can mean fewer purchases.

**Now reads:** The jam study from the fourth article, in which 30% of shoppers who stopped at a display of 6 jams bought a jar against 3% of those who stopped at a display of 24, is the field's favorite evidence that more choice can mean fewer purchases.

**Why:** The jam study is Iyengar and Lepper (2000), which the fourth article lists as a source but this article does not: of shoppers who stopped at the tasting table, 30% bought a jar from 6 jams and 3% from 24. Calling it the field's favorite evidence is fair; it has about four times the citations of either meta-analysis cited in the paragraph, and a 2024 review calls it the classic and seminal example of choice overload. Replication attempts and field data from retail, pensions, health plans, search engines and a mental health app show the effect is conditional, which the next sentences already say, so the only fix is to cite the paper here. Now cites [Iyengar & Lepper, When Choice Is Demotivating: Can One Desire Too Much of a Good Thing?, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995).

### Tool: statcheck: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** statcheck, described by its authors as a spellchecker for statistics, finds APA-style significance tests, recalculates each p-value and flags the ones that don't match.

**Now reads:** The tool behind that study, described by its authors as a spellchecker for statistics. It finds significance tests reported in APA style, recalculates each p-value and flags the ones that don't match. It usually can't read statistics in tables, so a clean result covers only the tests it found. Run a paper through the web version before your team builds on it.

**Why:** statcheck does what the article says, and its authors do call it a spellchecker for statistics (on statcheck.io rather than in the cited paper). Its accuracy is high, but the tool's own FAQ says it usually can't read statistics in tables, so a clean report is not a full check. Readers told to run a paper through it should know that. Now cites [statcheck.io (web version and FAQ)](https://statcheck.io).
