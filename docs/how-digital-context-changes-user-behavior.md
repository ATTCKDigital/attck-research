# Evidence review: How Digital Context Changes User Behavior

Article: [How Digital Context Changes User Behavior](https://attck.com/blog/how-digital-context-changes-user-behavior/)  
Reviewed: November 2026 · Method version 2 · [How these reviews work](https://github.com/ATTCKDigital/attck-research/blob/main/.claude/skills/evidence-review/METHOD.md)

## How this review works

Each factual claim in the article was checked against the source it cites, then challenged by reviewers whose job was to find the strongest evidence against it: replication attempts, meta-analyses, later papers that cite the original work, and research from other fields that study the same mechanism. A separate reviewer weighed both sides, and a script re-checked every quoted source. Where a claim was overstated or misattributed, the article was corrected. The research below supports the article as it now reads.

## Background research

### Introduction

**Behind a device, users tend to be less patient and more willing to walk away. The screen removes most of the social cost of leaving.**

Browsing logs put the median page visit near 9 seconds (Weinreich et al. 2006) and the stay-or-leave decision in the first 10 (Nielsen 2011), and a survey-mode experiment shows that removing the human interviewer lowers social pressure (Kreuter et al. 2008). One boundary: the same grocery households are less price-sensitive and more brand-loyal online than in store (Chu et al. 2008), so screens change patience more than they change diligence.

- [Nielsen, How Long Do Users Stay on Web Pages? (NN/g, 2011, summarizing Liu, White & Dumais 2010)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/): “It's clear from the chart that the first 10 seconds of the page visit are critical for users' decision to stay or leave.”
- [Kreuter, Presser & Tourangeau, Social Desirability Bias in CATI, IVR, and Web Surveys, Public Opinion Quarterly 72(5) (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfn063): “Web administration increased the level of reporting of sensitive information and reporting accuracy relative to conventional CATI, with IVR intermediate between the other two modes.”
- [Krishnan & Sitaraman, Video Stream Quality Impacts Viewer Behavior, IEEE/ACM Transactions on Networking (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1109/tnet.2013.2281542): “viewers start to abandon a video if it takes more than 2 s to start up, with each incremental delay of 1 s resulting in a 5.8% increase in the abandonment rate.”

### The Online Disinhibition Effect

**Psychologists use the term "Online Disinhibition Effect" for social behavior such as trolling or over-sharing. The effect has not been tested on buying decisions, but a similar logic plausibly applies: in a digital context, the social cost of walking away is close to zero.**

Suler (2004) attributes online disinhibition to six factors, including invisibility and asynchronicity. A 2026 meta-analysis of 80 studies confirms the toxic side (anonymity and disinhibition correlate with cyber aggression), and a survey-mode experiment shows that removing the human interviewer raises candid reporting (Kreuter et al. 2008), the mechanism the article leans on. The self-disclosure side is more mixed (Ruppel et al. 2016).

- [Suler, The Online Disinhibition Effect, CyberPsychology & Behavior 7(3), 2004 (abstract, PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15257832/): “While online, some people self-disclose or act out more frequently or intensely than they would in person.”
- [Anonymity, Online Disinhibition, and Cyber Aggression: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis, Trauma, Violence, & Abuse (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1177/15248380261476605): “anonymity and online disinhibition were both significantly and positively correlated with cyber aggression”
- [Kreuter, Presser & Tourangeau, Social Desirability Bias in CATI, IVR, and Web Surveys, Public Opinion Quarterly 72(5) (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfn063): “Web administration increased the level of reporting of sensitive information and reporting accuracy relative to conventional CATI, with IVR intermediate between the other two modes.”

### System 1 Dominance

**Daniel Kahneman, a Nobel laureate, distinguishes System 1 (fast, automatic, intuitive) from System 2 (slow, calculating, logical) thinking.**

The System 1 / System 2 labels were introduced by Keith Stanovich and Richard West in 2000 and popularized by Kahneman's 2011 book, which credits them; Kahneman's own 2003 paper draws the same distinction between effortless intuition and deliberate reasoning. He shared the 2002 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences.

- [Kahneman, A perspective on judgment and choice: Mapping bounded rationality, American Psychologist 58(9) (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.58.9.697): “a distinction between effortless intuition and deliberate reasoning.”
- [Wikipedia, Daniel Kahneman (accessed 2026-11-11)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Kahneman): “awarded the 2002 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences together with Vernon L. Smith.”
- [Stanovich & West, Behavioral and Brain Sciences 23 (2000), p. 658](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~mckenzie/StanovichBBS.pdf): “System 1 is characterized as automatic, largely unconscious, and relatively undemanding of computational capacity.”

**Much of everyday web use, especially on familiar sites, runs on fast, intuitive System 1 processing. First impressions of a page form within tens of milliseconds, and users rely on mental shortcuts and familiar patterns rather than reading an interface line by line. When a layout breaks a familiar pattern, users often react before they can say why; on a new or unusual site, they slow down and think.**

Lab studies show visual complexity and prototypicality shape aesthetic ratings of web pages within 50 ms and even 17 ms (Tuch et al. 2012), and judgments of usability and trust are largely driven by that appeal (Lindgaard et al. 2011). Diary studies of habits add the boundary: actions in stable, familiar contexts run without conscious guidance, while new contexts bring thought back (Wood et al. 2002).

- [Tuch, Presslaber, Stoecklin, Opwis & Bargas-Avila, IJHCS 70(11) (2012)](https://research.google/pubs/pub38315/): “Results suggest that VC and PT affect aesthetic perception even within 17ms,”
- [Wood, Quinn & Kashy, Habits in everyday life: Thought, emotion, and action, JPSP 83(6) (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.83.6.1281): “they were likely to think about issues unrelated to their behavior, presumably because they did not have to consciously guide their actions.”
- [Lindgaard, Dudek, Sen, Sumegi & Noonan, ACM TOCHI 18(1) (2011, abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1959022.1959023): “The results suggest that all three types of judgments are largely driven by visual appeal”

### The Back Button Safety Net

**In the digital world, the Back button provides a low-cost escape hatch. Choices that are easy to undo get weaker commitment, and web browsing shows it: a quarter of page views last under four seconds, and about seven in ten online shopping carts are abandoned. When users hit friction, leaving costs them little, so each added obstacle raises the odds that they go.**

Experiments on reversible choices found that people who could change their minds valued their choice less (Gilbert & Ebert 2002), which is the mechanism behind 'superficial commitment'. Browsing logs show a quarter of pages are viewed for under four seconds (Weinreich et al. 2006), and Baymard's aggregate of 50 studies puts average cart abandonment near 70%. A meta-analysis of return policies adds a twist: easy reversal also makes people more willing to buy.

- [Gilbert & Ebert, Decisions and revisions: The affective forecasting of changeable outcomes, JPSP 82(4) (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.82.4.503): “those who had the opportunity to change their minds liked their prints less than those who did not”
- [Weinreich et al., Off the Beaten Tracks (WWW 2006)](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2006/programme/files/xhtml/18/p018-weinreich/p018-weinreich.html): “25% of all documents were displayed for less than 4 seconds”
- [Baymard Institute, Cart Abandonment Rate statistics (2025)](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate): “we've calculated the average cart abandonment rate of 70.22%.”

### Pull quote / Scanning Over Reading

**People rarely read web pages word by word; they scan, picking out individual words and sentences (Jakob Nielsen).**

Nielsen's 1997 finding that 79% of test users scanned new pages has independent support: eye-tracking of online newspaper readers found they scan more and read less than print readers (Holmqvist et al. 2003), a decade-long survey of reading habits describes screen reading as browsing, scanning and keyword spotting (Liu 2005), and a meta-analysis of 54 studies finds comprehension on screens falls behind paper, most of all under time limits (Delgado et al. 2018).

- [Nielsen, How Users Read on the Web (NN/g, 1997)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/): “People rarely read Web pages word by word; instead, they scan the page, picking out individual words and sentences.”
- [Holmqvist, Holsanova, Barthelson & Lundqvist, Reading or scanning? A study of newspaper and net paper reading, in The Mind's Eye (Elsevier, 2003)](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/publication/793151): “This paper presents results showing that in fact net paper readers scan more and read less than newspaper readers.”
- [Delgado, Vargas, Ackerman & Salmeron, Don't throw away your printed books: A meta-analysis on the effects of reading media on reading comprehension, Educational Research Review 25 (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.003): “the paper-based reading advantage increased in time-constrained reading compared to self-paced reading”

### Scanning Over Reading

**Instead of reading, users engage in 'information foraging'.**

Pirolli and Card's information foraging theory (Psychological Review, 1999) models people as adaptive foragers who follow 'information scent' toward whatever looks likely to pay off. Later peer-reviewed work applies the same framework to skimming within a text, where readers move on once a paragraph's information rate drops (Duggan & Payne 2009), and to consumers reading online reviews (2021).

- [Trepess, Information Foraging Theory, Glossary of Human Computer Interaction (Interaction Design Foundation)](https://ixdf.org/literature/book/the-glossary-of-human-computer-interaction/information-foraging-theory): “Information Foraging is a theory that describes information retrieval behaviour proposed by Pirolli and Card (1999).”
- [Duggan & Payne, Text skimming: the process and effectiveness of foraging through text under time pressure, J. Exp. Psychol. Applied 15(3) (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016995): “These findings were interpreted as evidence in support of a "satisficing" account of skimming process.”
- [A quick bite and instant gratification: A simulated Yelp experiment on consumer review information foraging behavior, Information Processing & Management 58(1) (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ipm.2020.102391): “many heuristic consumer behaviors are better explained by behavioral ecology models, such as information foraging.”

**Eye-tracking studies reveal scanning patterns such as the "F-pattern" on dense, unformatted text and the "layer-cake" pattern on pages with clear headings. In both, users fixate on headings and the start of paragraphs and skip dense blocks of text. A value proposition buried deep in a paragraph is easy to miss.**

NN/g's 2006 study recorded 232 users across thousands of pages, and an independent HFES study confirmed the F-pattern on text pages while finding a different path on image pages (Shrestha et al. 2007). Eye-tracking of skim readers shows attention concentrates at the start of paragraphs and the top of pages (Duggan & Payne 2009). NN/g's own 2017 revisit stresses that good formatting reduces F-scanning, and the layer-cake pattern (Pernice 2019) is what scanning looks like when headings do their job.

- [Nielsen, F-Shaped Pattern For Reading Web Content (NN/g, 2006)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/): “In our new eyetracking study, we recorded how 232 users looked at thousands of Web pages.”
- [Shrestha, Lenz, Chaparro & Owens, 'F' Pattern Scanning of Text and Images in Web Pages, Proceedings of the HFES Annual Meeting 51(18) (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1177/154193120705101831): “Nielsen's 'F' pattern (2006) was confirmed in both the text-browse and text-search tasks”

### The Search for Trust Proxies

**Judgments of a page's visual appeal form in as little as 50 milliseconds.**

Lindgaard's 50 ms finding has been replicated and extended: visual complexity and prototypicality shape aesthetic ratings at 17 ms (Tuch et al. 2012), 548 raters judging 450 sites at 500 ms gave stable appeal ratings (Reinecke et al. 2013), and the same speed appears for faces, paintings and ads. The snap judgment is about looks: it shapes trust impressions (Lindgaard et al. 2011) but does not predict how usable a site proves in use (Thielsch et al. 2015).

- [Lindgaard, Fernandes, Dudek & Brown, Behaviour & Information Technology 25(2), 2006](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1080/01449290500330448): “Thus, visual appeal can be assessed within 50 ms, suggesting that web designers have about 50 ms to make a good first impression.”
- [Tuch, Presslaber, Stoecklin, Opwis & Bargas-Avila, IJHCS 70(11) (2012)](https://research.google/pubs/pub38315/): “Results suggest that VC and PT affect aesthetic perception even within 17ms,”
- [Reinecke, Yeh, Miratrix, Mardiko et al., Predicting users' first impressions of website aesthetics (CHI 2013)](https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2481281): “Users make lasting judgments about a website's appeal within a split second of seeing it for the first time.”

**In the absence of a human face or a handshake, the brain scans for "trust proxies." Users lean most on visual design, then on social proof such as reviews and client logos; few understand technical signals like the browser padlock. If a site looks outdated or broken, users trust it less, even if the backend is secure.**

In a 2002 Stanford study of 2,684 people, the 'design look' of a site was the most frequently mentioned factor in credibility judgments, present in 46.1% of comments, and a 2022 meta-analysis of 147 studies finds aesthetics among the strongest correlates of judged information quality (Zhang & Kim). The padlock is a weaker cue than it looks: a 1,880-person survey found 89% misunderstand it, and Chrome dropped the lock icon in 2023.

- [Fogg, Soohoo, Danielsen et al., How Do People Evaluate a Web Site's Credibility? (Stanford / Consumer Reports WebWatch, 2002)](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/research/how-do-people-evaluate-a-web-sites-credibility): “We found that the "design look" of the site was mentioned most frequently, being present in 46.1 percent of the comments.”
- [Zhang & Kim, Consumers' Evaluation of Web-Based Health Information Quality: Meta-analysis, JMIR (2022)](https://doi.org/10.2196/36463): “Among the antecedents of web-based health information quality, system navigability (ρ=0.56), aesthetics (ρ=0.49), and ease of understanding (ρ=0.49) had the strongest relationships with web-based health information quality.”
- [Lindgaard, Dudek, Sen, Sumegi & Noonan, ACM TOCHI 18(1) (2011, abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1959022.1959023): “The results suggest that all three types of judgments are largely driven by visual appeal”

### Front-Load Value

**Journalists have long used the "inverted pyramid" style of writing, and it suits task-focused web content well. Lead with the point on pages people scan; save narrative build-up for complex explanations, where it can inform readers better.**

NN/g recommends the inverted pyramid because scanners can stop at any point and still leave with the main point, and its eye-tracking finds users spend about 57% of viewing time above the fold (Fessenden 2018). A web experiment on a news site found inverted-pyramid stories were read faster and recalled better than narrative ones (DeAngelo & Yegiyan 2018). Journalism studies add a boundary: for complex science news, narrative structure can raise interest and understanding (Yaros 2006, 2011).

- [Schade, Inverted Pyramid: Writing for Comprehension (NN/g, 2018)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/inverted-pyramid/): “This style of writing is perfectly suited to writing for the web.”
- [DeAngelo & Yegiyan, Looking for Efficiency: How Online News Structure and Emotional Tone Influence Processing Time and Memory, Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1077699018792272): “Participants spent less time reading stories with an inverted pyramid structure yet recalled these stories better than stories in a narrative structure”
- [Fessenden, Scrolling and Attention (NN/g, 2018)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/): “In our most recent study, users spent about 57% of their page-viewing time above the fold.”

### Use Explicit Visual Signifiers

**If a user needs to click something, it should look clickable. Use strong, conventional signifiers: underlined or colored links, filled or bordered buttons, contrasting color, and familiar placement. Depth and shadow help most for older users; in an NN/g eye-tracking study, pages with weak signifiers took users 22% longer to find the target.**

NN/g's 2017 eye-tracking study of 71 users found weak (flat) signifiers caused 22% slower target-finding, and a peer-reviewed study found flat design slower or less accurate at identifying clickable objects for older adults, with 'click uncertainty' even among younger adults (Urbano et al. 2020). Other controlled comparisons find no usability difference between flat and skeuomorphic designs, which suggests convention and contrast, not visual depth, are what matter.

- [Meyer, Flat UI Elements Attract Less Attention and Cause Uncertainty (NN/g, 2017)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/flat-ui-less-attention-cause-uncertainty/): “On average participants spent 22% more time (i.e., slower task performance) looking at the pages with weak signifiers.”
- [Urbano, Guerreiro & Nicolau, From skeuomorphism to flat design: age-related differences in performance and aesthetic perceptions, Behaviour & Information Technology (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1080/0144929X.2020.1814867): “Results show flat design is either slower or less accurate than traditional skeuomorph interfaces for older adults across three tasks: visual search, identifying clickable objects, and multiple page navigation.”
- [Chen, Huang & Zhou, Skeuomorphic or flat icons for an efficient visual search by younger and older adults?, Applied Ergonomics (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2020.103073): “The results imply that the younger participants could use the skeuomorphic icons more efficiently than they could use the flat icons”

### Reduce Cognitive Load

**Every field in a form, every extra banner, and every pop-up adds work for the user. Remove anything the immediate transaction does not need, and test before adding steps that serve the business rather than the buyer. If a phone number is optional, drop the field or mark it clearly as optional and say why you ask. Delay counts too: controlled tests show that even a tenth of a second of extra load time measurably cuts revenue.**

Burden effects on completion replicate outside UX: a randomized web survey found that longer announced questionnaires cut both starts and completions (Galesic & Bosnjak 2009), and the H&R Block FAFSA experiment found that help with the form, not information, raised college enrolment (Bettinger et al. 2012). Baymard's checkout testing found that a required phone field with no explanation led some participants to enter false numbers or abandon the purchase. Controlled slowdowns at Bing showed each 100 ms of extra load time cuts revenue by about 0.6%.

- [Galesic & Bosnjak, Effects of Questionnaire Length on Participation and Indicators of Response Quality in a Web Survey, Public Opinion Quarterly 73(2) (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfp031): “As expected, the longer the stated length, the fewer respondents started and completed the questionnaire.”
- [Bettinger, Long, Oreopoulos & Sanbonmatsu, The H&R Block FAFSA Experiment, QJE 127(3), 2012 (NBER w15361)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15361): “However, only providing aid eligibility information without also giving assistance with the form had no significant effect on FAFSA submission rates.”
- [Baymard Institute, Phone Number UX: Always Explain Why the 'Phone Field' Is Required (2020)](https://baymard.com/blog/explain-phone-number-field): “some participants in our testing provided false information or abandoned the checkout process altogether”

### Case Study

**By Spool's account, the site saw a 45% increase in purchases and an estimated extra $300 million in revenue in the first year. Results of that size are rare, and a later field experiment found that an optional sign-up request at the start of shopping did not hurt sales. The lesson is narrower: do not force account creation at checkout, which remains one of the top reasons shoppers abandon a purchase.**

Spool's case comes from an unnamed retailer he describes only as 'the $25 billion retailer', and his 2011 back story explains the figures were pieced together from partly uninstrumented analytics. Independent survey data back the direction: Baymard finds 18% of US shoppers have abandoned a cart because the site wanted them to create an account. A 2021 randomized field experiment adds a boundary: an optional registration request at the start of shopping did not hurt conversion and raised long-run purchases, so the problem is forced registration at checkout, not registration as such.

- [Spool, The $300 Million Button (UIE / Center Centre, 2009)](https://articles.centercentre.com/three_hund_million_button/): “The number of customers purchasing went up by 45%.”
- [Baymard Institute, Cart Abandonment Rate statistics (2025)](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate): “18% The site wanted me to create an account”

## What changed in the article

These edits were made on November 12, 2026 after the review.

### Introduction: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Behind a screen, people become significantly less patient, more skeptical and more ruthlessly efficient than they are in a physical store; the screen strips away social friction.

**Now reads:** Behind a device, users tend to be less patient and more willing to walk away. The screen removes most of the social cost of leaving.

**Why:** The impatience half is well supported: most page visits last seconds, the stay-or-leave decision falls in the first ten, and abandonment climbs about 6% per second of delay. The 'social friction' half now has support too, since removing a human interviewer makes people more candid. But the only like-for-like comparison of the same shoppers in store and online found them less price-sensitive and more brand-loyal online, and supermarket shoppers are also fast and inattentive, so 'significantly' and 'ruthlessly efficient' should go. Now cites [Nielsen, How Long Do Users Stay on Web Pages? (NN/g, 2011)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/).

### The Online Disinhibition Effect: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** The Online Disinhibition Effect describes social behavior online (trolling, over-sharing), and it applies equally to decision-making.

**Now reads:** Psychologists use the term "Online Disinhibition Effect" for social behavior such as trolling or over-sharing. The effect has not been tested on buying decisions, but a similar logic plausibly applies: in a digital context, the social cost of walking away is close to zero.

**Why:** The attribution is right: Suler's 2004 paper is about people self-disclosing or acting out more online. But the effect is uneven even on its home ground: an 80-study meta-analysis confirms the aggression side, while a meta-analysis of self-disclosure found people disclose more face to face than online and anonymity correlates only weakly with disclosure. None of 4,591 papers citing Suler tests the effect on purchasing, impatience or abandonment, so the 'applies equally to decision-making' extension should be presented as the author's analogy.

### System 1 Dominance: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** The vast majority of digital navigation is a System 1 activity: users decide in milliseconds using heuristics, do not 'read' interfaces but 'feel' them, and reject a layout that breaks a mental model before consciously understanding why.

**Now reads:** Much of everyday web use, especially on familiar sites, runs on fast, intuitive System 1 processing. First impressions of a page form within tens of milliseconds, and users rely on mental shortcuts and familiar patterns rather than reading an interface line by line. When a layout breaks a familiar pattern, users often react before they can say why; on a new or unusual site, they slow down and think.

**Why:** The kernel holds: first impressions of a page form within tens of milliseconds, and habit research shows routine actions in familiar settings run without conscious guidance. But no study measures what share of navigation is 'System 1', and the same habit research finds thought tracks action in new or shifting contexts, so the claim applies at most to familiar sites. The best-validated model of web navigation has users read and evaluate link text one at a time and decide by satisficing, which contradicts 'they do not read interfaces', and navigation choices take seconds to minutes, not milliseconds. Now cites [Tuch et al., The role of visual complexity and prototypicality regarding first impression of websites, IJHCS 2012](https://research.google/pubs/pub38315/).

### The Back Button Safety Net: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** The Back button acts as a low-cost escape hatch that encourages rapid exploration and superficial commitment; users abandon a path the moment they meet friction.

**Now reads:** In the digital world, the Back button provides a low-cost escape hatch. Choices that are easy to undo get weaker commitment, and web browsing shows it: a quarter of page views last under four seconds, and about seven in ten online shopping carts are abandoned. When users hit friction, leaving costs them little, so each added obstacle raises the odds that they go.

**Why:** The low-commitment mechanism now has experimental backing: people who can reverse a choice like it less than people whose choice is final. Rapid, shallow browsing (a quarter of page views under four seconds) and about 70% cart abandonment are documented. But a cheap escape hatch also raises commitment to buy (lenient return policies increase purchases, by meta-analysis), going back is an evaluated decision rather than a reflex, abandonment rises step by step with delay rather than 'the moment' friction appears, and most abandonment is intent-driven. Keep the point, drop the absolutes, and mark '500 options in thirty seconds' as illustrative. Now cites [Gilbert & Ebert, Decisions and revisions: The affective forecasting of changeable outcomes, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 82(4), 2002](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.82.4.503).

### Scanning Over Reading: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Eye-tracking studies consistently reveal F-pattern or layer-cake scanning, where users fixate on headlines, first sentences and bulleted lists while ignoring dense blocks of text; a value proposition buried in the third sentence of a paragraph effectively does not exist.

**Now reads:** Eye-tracking studies reveal scanning patterns such as the "F-pattern" on dense, unformatted text and the "layer-cake" pattern on pages with clear headings. In both, users fixate on headings and the start of paragraphs and skip dense blocks of text. A value proposition buried deep in a paragraph is easy to miss.

**Why:** Both cited NN/g studies exist, the F-pattern has an independent peer-reviewed replication on text pages, and skim readers do dwell on the start of paragraphs and pages, which backs the 'first sentences' detail the cited sources never state. But 'consistently' is wrong by the cited authors' own account: the F-pattern appears on dense unformatted text, image pages produce a different path, a vendor re-analysis of 99 sites found no F at all, and skimming was no better than reading half of each paragraph, so 'the third sentence effectively does not exist' overstates. Now cites [Duggan & Payne, Text skimming: the process and effectiveness of foraging through text under time pressure, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied 15(3), 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016995).

### The Search for Trust Proxies: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Without a human face, users look for trust proxies (high-fidelity design, social proof, HTTPS padlocks); if a site looks outdated or broken, users perceive a security risk even if the backend is secure.

**Now reads:** In the absence of a human face or a handshake, the brain scans for "trust proxies." Users lean most on visual design, then on social proof such as reviews and client logos; few understand technical signals like the browser padlock. If a site looks outdated or broken, users trust it less, even if the backend is secure.

**Why:** The design-as-trust-proxy half is well supported: 'design look' was the most frequent basis for credibility judgments in Fogg's 2,684-person study, and a 147-study meta-analysis finds aesthetics among the strongest correlates of judged information quality. The HTTPS padlock example is out of date as well as weak: 89% of users misunderstand it and Chrome removed the lock icon in 2023 because only 11% understood it and nearly all phishing sites display it. 'Perceive a security risk' is contradicted by a lab experiment in which poor system quality left threat appraisals unchanged, so say users trust the site less. Now cites [Fogg et al., How Do People Evaluate a Web Site's Credibility? (Stanford Persuasive Technology Lab / Consumer Reports WebWatch, 2002)](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/research/how-do-people-evaluate-a-web-sites-credibility).

### Front-Load Value: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Journalists' inverted-pyramid style (conclusion first) is essential for UX writing.

**Now reads:** Journalists have long used the "inverted pyramid" style of writing, and it suits task-focused web content well. Lead with the point on pages people scan; save narrative build-up for complex explanations, where it can inform readers better.

**Why:** The cited NN/g article does recommend the inverted pyramid for web writing, and the experimental record is more favourable than v1 suggested: in free-choice reading on a news website, inverted-pyramid stories were read faster and recalled better than narrative ones, chronological stories cost more effort with no memory gain, and readers prefer the pyramid. The boundary is real, though: for complex science news, narrative structure produced more interest and understanding in two web experiments. 'Essential for UX' overstates a practitioner piece that cites no study; scope it to task-focused, scanned content. Now cites [DeAngelo & Yegiyan, Looking for Efficiency: How Online News Structure and Emotional Tone Influence Processing Time and Memory, Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/1077699018792272).

### Use Explicit Visual Signifiers: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** A flat text link requires cognitive effort to identify as clickable; a raised, high-contrast button signals clickability intuitively.

**Now reads:** If a user needs to click something, it should look clickable. Use strong, conventional signifiers: underlined or colored links, filled or bordered buttons, contrasting color, and familiar placement. Depth and shadow help most for older users; in an NN/g eye-tracking study, pages with weak signifiers took users 22% longer to find the target.

**Why:** Clear signifiers do help: pages with weak signifiers took 22% longer in NN/g's 71-user eye-tracking study, flat design was slower or less accurate at identifying clickable objects for older adults with 'click uncertainty' even among younger ones, and skeuomorphic icons were searched more efficiently. But depth is not the active ingredient: flat controls outperformed in a smart-home study, flat and skeuomorphic objects evoke the same affordance when measured perceptuomotorically, depth effects vanish under low load, and the conventional underlined link the article dismisses is itself a strong signifier. Say 'clear, conventional signifiers' and drop 'a text link requires cognitive effort to identify'. Now cites [Meyer, Flat UI Elements Attract Less Attention and Cause Uncertainty (NN/g, 2017)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/flat-ui-less-attention-cause-uncertainty/).

### Reduce Cognitive Load: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Every extra form field, banner and pop-up increases cognitive load, and every extra millisecond of friction increases the likelihood that users go back; optional fields such as phone number should be removed.

**Now reads:** Every field in a form, every extra banner, and every pop-up adds work for the user. Remove anything the immediate transaction does not need, and test before adding steps that serve the business rather than the buyer. If a phone number is optional, drop the field or mark it clearly as optional and say why you ask. Delay counts too: controlled tests show that even a tenth of a second of extra load time measurably cuts revenue.

**Why:** The direction is well supported, now beyond practitioner research: a randomized web survey experiment found that the longer the announced questionnaire, the fewer people started and finished, a randomized FAFSA experiment found that cutting form burden raised take-up where information alone did nothing, and Baymard's checkout testing found required phone fields caused false entries and abandonment. The absolutes overreach: 'every extra millisecond' has controlled evidence only for page load time (0.6% revenue per 100 ms at Bing), not for fields or banners, and a 75,000-user field experiment found an added registration request did not cut short-term conversion and raised long-run purchases, so 'remove everything not 100% necessary' is too strong. Now cites [Baymard Institute, Phone Number UX: Always Explain Why the 'Phone Field' Is Required (2020)](https://baymard.com/blog/explain-phone-number-field).

### The 5-Second Test: not supported

**Claim reviewed:** The 5-second test is an evidence-based method that mimics the actual processing time a user grants a new digital experience before deciding to stay or bounce.

**Now reads:** If people cannot name the primary value proposition after the test, the first impression needs work. Copy further down the page will not rescue it, because many visitors decide whether to stay before they ever reach it.

**Why:** The 5-Second Test is a real, widely used technique, but the sentence makes two claims the evidence does not bear. Large dwell-time logs put the real stay-or-leave window at about ten seconds (median visit 9.4 seconds) and visual impressions form within 50 milliseconds, so a fixed five seconds matches neither; the one peer-reviewed experiment that tests the rule directly, at 2, 5 and 10 seconds, concludes it 'misrepresents the reality of human cognition' because results depend on working memory and page complexity. None of the 11 papers that cite that experiment re-tests the window, and 'evidence-based' rests on no retrievable validation: the method's originator gives no empirical derivation of five seconds, and the one validation study (2016) cannot be read from any source.

### Case Study: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Jared Spool documented a major e-commerce retailer that replaced a forced Register/Log In step with a Continue button plus the line 'You do not need to create an account to make purchases', producing a 45% increase in purchases and $300 million extra revenue in the first year.

**Now reads:** By Spool's account, the site saw a 45% increase in purchases and an estimated extra $300 million in revenue in the first year. Results of that size are rare, and a later field experiment found that an optional sign-up request at the start of shopping did not hurt sales. The lesson is narrower: do not force account creation at checkout, which remains one of the top reasons shoppers abandon a purchase.

**Why:** The retelling is faithful to Spool: the Register button became Continue, purchases rose 45%, and the site 'saw an additional $300,000,000' in year one. But 'documented' overstates, since the retailer is unnamed, the figures were relayed by the client's CEO, and Spool's own back story says the analytics were not fully instrumented. The closing generalisation that removing friction yields 'exponential' results is now contradicted, not just unsupported: a randomized field experiment with about 75,000 new shoppers found an up-front registration request did not cut short-term conversion and raised long-run purchases by about 11%. Attribute the figures to Spool, drop 'exponential', and keep the lesson narrow: do not force account creation at checkout, which remains one of the top reasons shoppers abandon. Now cites [Huang, Mojumder, Sun, Lv & Golden, Not Registered? Please Sign Up First: A Randomized Field Experiment on the Ex Ante Registration Request, Information Systems Research 32(3), 2021](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/orisre/v32y2021i3p914-931.html).
