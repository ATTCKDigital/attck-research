# Evidence review: How Users Build Trust in Brands

Article: [How Users Build Trust in Brands](https://attck.com/blog/how-users-build-trust-in-brands/)  
Reviewed: November 2026 · Method version 2 · [How these reviews work](https://github.com/ATTCKDigital/attck-research/blob/main/.claude/skills/evidence-review/METHOD.md)

## How this review works

Each factual claim in the article was checked against the source it cites, then challenged by reviewers whose job was to find the strongest evidence against it: replication attempts, meta-analyses, later papers that cite the original work, and research from other fields that study the same mechanism. A separate reviewer weighed both sides, and a script re-checked every quoted source. Where a claim was overstated or misattributed, the article was corrected. The research below supports the article as it now reads.

## Background research

### Introduction

**Consumer WebWatch, a project of Consumers Union, wrote five areas into its guidelines for improving web credibility: say who runs the site, label the advertising, offer customer service, publish corrections, post a privacy policy.**

- [Fogg et al., How Do People Evaluate a Web Site's Credibility?, Consumer WebWatch / Stanford PTL, 2002 (full report PDF)](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/05/stanfordPTL.pdf): “Consumer WebWatch’s five general guidelines for improving credibility on the Web: Identity, Advertising and Sponsorships, Customer Service, Corrections, and Privacy.”

**In 2002, B.J. Fogg's Stanford lab worked with Consumer WebWatch and asked 2,684 people to judge the credibility of real websites and say why.**

- [Fogg et al., 2002 (Consumer Reports Advocacy landing page)](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/research/how-do-people-evaluate-a-web-sites-credibility): “In this study, 2,684 people evaluated the credibility of two live Web sites on a similar topic (such as health or news).”

**The site's design look came up in 46.1% of the comments, more than anything else.**

Confirmed in the report. Later experiments (Lindgaard et al. 2011) found snap trustworthiness judgments of homepages are largely driven by visual appeal.

- [Lindgaard, Dudek, Sen, Sumegi & Noonan, ACM TOCHI 18(1), 2011 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1959022.1959023): “The results suggest that all three types of judgments are largely driven by visual appeal”

**The authors wrote: "We found a mismatch, as in other areas of life, between what people say is important and what they actually do."**

Quote confirmed verbatim. The 'say' side comes from Consumer WebWatch's separate 2002 poll, which the report contrasts with this study.

- [Barth & de Jong, The privacy paradox, Telematics and Informatics 34(7), 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tele.2017.04.013): “While users claim to be very concerned about their privacy, they nevertheless undertake very little to protect their personal data.”

### What People Are Actually Trusting

**Mayer, Davis and Schoorman (1995) define trust as "the willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the expectation that the other will perform a particular action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that other party."**

The publisher page is paywalled; the definition was confirmed as quoted in an open-access review.

- [Trust and interaction design in AI-enabled systems: a systematic literature review, Frontiers in Psychology, 2026 (PMC13619576), quoting Mayer et al. 1995](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13619576/): “willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the expectation that the other will perform a particular action important to the trustor”

**The Mayer paper names three qualities that recur in the literature as grounds for trust: ability, benevolence and integrity.**

Confirmed second-hand through the Colquitt et al. (2007) meta-analysis abstract.

- [Colquitt, Scott & LePine, J Appl Psychol 92(4), 2007 (PubMed abstract)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17638454/): “The trust literature distinguishes trustworthiness (the ability, benevolence, and integrity of a trustee) and trust propensity (a dispositional willingness to rely on others) from trust”

**It pays. In data on 107 brands, Arjun Chaudhuri and Morris Holbrook found that brand trust and brand affect together were tied to two kinds of loyalty: repeat purchase, which went with higher market share, and attitudinal loyalty, which went with a higher relative price.**

The study uses survey data from one point in time and separates purchase loyalty (linked to market share) from attitudinal loyalty (linked to relative price). A later longitudinal experiment found trust's effect on loyalty grew over time.

- [Chaudhuri & Holbrook, Journal of Marketing 65(2), 2001 (Crossref abstract, source)](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1509/jmkg.65.2.81.18255): “brand trust and brand affect combine to determine purchase loyalty and attitudinal loyalty. Purchase loyalty, in turn, leads to greater market share, and attitudinal loyalty leads to a higher relative price”

### Finding 1

**Sillence and colleagues followed 15 women facing a risky health decision as they searched the web for advice over four consecutive weeks.**

- [Sillence, Briggs, Fishwick & Harris, CHI 2004 (OpenAlex abstract, source)](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1145/985692.985776): “Fifteen women faced with a risky health decision were observed while searching the Internet for information and advice over four consecutive weeks.”

**Their spoken comments supported a staged model: design appeal predicted rejection, while selection (trusting a site's advice) was predicted by the credibility of its information and how personal the content felt.**

Qualitative content analysis of 15 participants; 'predicted' is descriptive, not statistical. A 2023 study with about 600 participants found aesthetics also raised trust, not only drove rejection.

**That reconciles Fogg's numbers with common sense. Looks work as a filter. Poor design can lose trust in seconds, and good design can win a first hearing, but trust itself is earned by the content and by how the company behaves over time.**

Sillence and colleagues' own six-month follow-up of the same study describes a staged process: sites were rejected on poor design, and well-designed sites then had to earn trust with credible, personal content. Three studies with more than 1,100 web users found that aesthetics had the largest effect on first impressions while content was what drove people to revisit or recommend a site, and a two-week field experiment found the halo from good looks fades with use. Looks do raise initial trust on their own, so the article now says good design wins a first hearing rather than that it cannot earn trust.

- [Sillence, Briggs, Harris & Fishwick, How do patients evaluate and make use of online health information?, Social Science & Medicine, 2007 (Europe PMC abstract, PMID 17328998)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2007.01.012): “sometimes rejecting high-quality content because of poor design. Well-designed sites were then effectively interrogated for credible and personalized content before being designated trustworthy”
- [Thielsch, Blotenberg & Jaron, User Evaluation of Websites: From First Impression to Recommendation, Interacting with Computers, 2014 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1093/iwc/iwt033): “aesthetics had the largest influence on first impressions, while all three constructs had an impact on first and overall impressions. However, only content contributed significantly to the intention to revisit or recommend a website”
- [Sonderegger, Zbinden, Uebelbacher & Sauer, The influence of product aesthetics and usability over the course of time, Ergonomics, 2012 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1080/00140139.2012.672658): “the positive effect of an aesthetically appealing product on perceived usability, reported in many previous studies, began to wane with increasing exposure time”
- [Chang, Doll, van 't Wout, Frank & Sanfey, Seeing is believing: Trustworthiness as a dynamic belief, Cognitive Psychology, 2010 (Europe PMC abstract, PMID 20553763)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2010.03.001): “trustworthiness is a belief about probability of reciprocation based initially on implicit judgments, and then dynamically updated based on experiences”
- [Adopting information systems at work: a longitudinal examination of trust dynamics, antecedents, and outcomes, Behaviour & Information Technology, 2023 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1080/0144929x.2023.2196598): “Person factors were stronger predictors of trust in early phases, whereas system characteristics were stronger predictors later in the process”

### Finding 2

**Kim and colleagues found trust was repaired more successfully when the accused party apologized for a failure of competence but denied culpability for a failure of integrity.**

Confirmed in the PubMed abstract and consistent with a 2017 meta-analysis (36 samples, N = 4,731, d = 0.44, stronger for competence violations). A 2025 meta-analysis of human-robot studies found repair effects small overall, though apology was among the most effective strategies.

- [Kim, Ferrin, Cooper & Dirks, J Appl Psychol 89(1), 2004 (PubMed abstract, source)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14769123/): “trust was repaired more successfully when mistrusted parties (a) apologized for violations concerning matters of competence but denied culpability for violations concerning matters of integrity”
- [The trust repair effect of apology: A systematic review and meta-analysis, Advances in Psychological Science 25(7), 2017 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.3724/sp.j.1042.2017.01103): “相比于诚实型信任违背, 道歉对能力型信任违背有较好的修复效果”

**For a brand, the reading is narrow but useful. When an error is yours, say so plainly. Denial can look better in the moment, especially against a charge of dishonesty, but it becomes the worse choice once evidence of fault appears.**

Kim et al. found denial beat apology for integrity charges when no further evidence came out, and apology beat denial once evidence of guilt appeared.

- [Of first impressions, shattered trust, and apology, Frontiers in Psychology, 2025 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1654463): “the results showed a sustained positive effect of first impressions (specifically, the impact of apology over denial)”

### The Outage GitLab Ran in Public

**On January 31, 2017, a GitLab engineer trying to rebuild a lagging database replica just before midnight UTC ran a deletion on the primary production database instead; about 300 GB of data was gone before the command was stopped.**

- [GitLab, Postmortem of database outage of January 31, 2017 (source)](https://about.gitlab.com/blog/postmortem-of-database-outage-of-january-31/): “The engineer terminated the process a second or two after noticing their mistake, but at this point around 300 GB of data had already been removed.”

**Changes made between 17:20 UTC and midnight were lost for good: by the company's estimate roughly 5,000 projects, 5,000 comments and 700 new user accounts.**

Confirmed in the postmortem. Git repositories and wikis were not affected by the data loss.

**In Kim's terms, a competence failure met with an apology is the combination that repaired trust better than denial.**

Kim et al. compared apology with denial only. A 2017 meta-analysis found apology repairs competence violations better than integrity violations.

- [Kim, Ferrin, Cooper & Dirks, J Appl Psychol 89(1), 2004 (PubMed abstract, source)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14769123/): “apologized for violations concerning matters of competence”
- [The trust repair effect of apology: A systematic review and meta-analysis, Advances in Psychological Science 25(7), 2017 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.3724/sp.j.1042.2017.01103): “相比于诚实型信任违背, 道歉对能力型信任违背有较好的修复效果”

### Pull quote

**"When trust is lost, it can almost always be traced back to a breakdown in one of these three drivers": authenticity, logic and empathy.**

The quoted sentence appears in HBR's summary of the article, not in the authors' body text, which says broken trust 'usually' traces to one driver.

### Conclusion

**Trust is the slowest of the three to build, because it rests on behavior a user can only see over time, and it is usually quicker to lose than to win.**

The idea that trust is easier to destroy than to build traces to Paul Slovic's 1993 'asymmetry principle'. Later experiments found the asymmetry holds for firsthand experience but not always for information people only read about.

- [Vulnerability or resilience? Examining trust asymmetry..., Frontiers in Psychology, 2023 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1207453): “negative information had a greater effect in reducing trust compared to the enhancing effect of positive information”

## What changed in the article

These edits were made on November 12, 2026 after the review.

### What People Are Actually Trusting: unverified

**Claim reviewed:** A polished interface speaks mostly to ability; benevolence and integrity are earned through behavior.

**Now reads:** A polished interface makes a strong first impression, and in snap judgments people read good design as a sign of trustworthiness in general. But benevolence and integrity are hard to prove with visuals alone. They are earned through behavior.

**Why:** This is the author's own inference and has no source. No study has tested which of the three trust factors visual polish affects. Experiments do show that good looks raise overall trustworthiness ratings, not only judgments of competence, so 'mostly to the first' is a guess. Now cites [Lindgaard, Dudek, Sen, Sumegi & Noonan, An exploration of relations between visual appeal, trustworthiness and perceived usability of homepages, ACM TOCHI (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1959022.1959023).

### What People Are Actually Trusting: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** In data on 107 brands, Chaudhuri and Holbrook found that brand trust and brand affect together drove loyalty, and loyalty led in turn to higher market share and a higher relative price.

**Now reads:** It pays. In data on 107 brands, Arjun Chaudhuri and Morris Holbrook found that brand trust and brand affect together were tied to two kinds of loyalty: repeat purchase, which went with higher market share, and attitudinal loyalty, which went with a higher relative price.

**Why:** The paper does link brand trust and affect to loyalty and loyalty to market share and price, using 107 brands. But it splits loyalty in two: purchase loyalty goes with market share, and attitudinal loyalty with relative price. The data come from one point in time, so 'drove' and 'led' describe a modeled path, not demonstrated cause and effect.

### Finding 1: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Looks work as a filter: they can lose trust quickly but cannot earn it on their own (reconciling Fogg's numbers with Sillence).

**Now reads:** That reconciles Fogg's numbers with common sense. Looks work as a filter. Poor design can lose trust in seconds, and good design can win a first hearing, but trust itself is earned by the content and by how the company behaves over time.

**Why:** The authors of the cited study said the same thing in their six-month follow-up of the same women: poor design got good sites rejected, and well-designed sites then had to earn trust with credible, personal content. Larger studies agree that looks dominate first impressions while content drives whether people come back or recommend a site, and the halo from good looks fades with use. But looks alone do raise initial trust, and even real money in trust games, so 'cannot earn it on their own' is too strong; the fix is to say looks win a first hearing and content and behavior earn the trust. Now cites [Thielsch, Blotenberg & Jaron, User Evaluation of Websites: From First Impression to Recommendation, Interacting with Computers (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1093/iwc/iwt033).

### Finding 2: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** When later evidence showed guilt, having apologized worked better than having denied.

**Now reads:** For a brand, the reading is narrow but useful. When an error is yours, say so plainly. Denial can look better in the moment, especially against a charge of dishonesty, but it becomes the worse choice once evidence of fault appears.

**Why:** The finding is in the abstract: when later evidence showed guilt, an earlier apology beat an earlier denial. The article's gloss that 'denial only helps when you are genuinely innocent' is slightly too simple, because in the same study denial also beat apology for integrity charges before any later evidence appeared.

### The Outage GitLab Ran in Public: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** In Kim's terms, a competence failure met with an apology is the combination most likely to repair trust; GitLab's outage was a failure of ability and its response worked on integrity and benevolence.

**Now reads:** In Kim's terms, a competence failure met with an apology is the combination that repaired trust better than denial.

**Why:** Kim's study compared only apology against denial, so it shows an apology beats denial for a competence failure, not that it is the response 'most likely' to repair trust among all options. A meta-analysis backs apology for competence failures, but a broader one found repair effects modest overall. The article already says GitLab's outcome was not measured, which is the right hedge.

### Pull quote: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Frances Frei and Anne Morriss (Begin with Trust): "Trust has three drivers: authenticity, logic, and empathy. When trust is lost, it can almost always be traced back to a breakdown in one of them."

**Now reads:** "When trust is lost, it can almost always be traced back to a breakdown in one of these three drivers": authenticity, logic and empathy.

**Why:** The quoted idea is on the HBR page, but in the article's summary paragraph, which introduces it with 'the authors argue', and the wording differs: HBR says 'three core drivers of trust' and 'one of these three drivers'. The body text is softer, saying broken trust 'usually' traces to one driver. Pass 2 did not find the sentence; it is there in the page summary.

### Conclusion: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Trust is the slowest to build, because it rests on behavior seen over time, and the quickest to lose.

**Now reads:** Trust is the slowest of the three to build, because it rests on behavior a user can only see over time, and it is usually quicker to lose than to win.

**Why:** Trust being quicker to lose than to gain is a well-known idea from risk research, and experiments support it when people experience outcomes firsthand. But it is not universal: when people only read about good and bad outcomes, trust moved equally in both directions, and a 2026 review describes the asymmetry as dependent on context. The sentence has no source. Now cites [Slovic, Perceived Risk, Trust, and Democracy, Risk Analysis (1993)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6924.1993.tb01329.x).
