# Evidence review: Emotional Tone and User Trust

Article: [Emotional Tone and User Trust](https://attck.com/blog/emotional-tone-and-user-trust/)  
Reviewed: November 2026 · Method version 2 · [How these reviews work](https://github.com/ATTCKDigital/attck-research/blob/main/.claude/skills/evidence-review/METHOD.md)

## How this review works

Each factual claim in the article was checked against the source it cites, then challenged by reviewers whose job was to find the strongest evidence against it: replication attempts, meta-analyses, later papers that cite the original work, and research from other fields that study the same mechanism. A separate reviewer weighed both sides, and a script re-checked every quoted source. Where a claim was overstated or misattributed, the article was corrected. The research below supports the article as it now reads.

### Voice Stays Put, Tone Moves

**Mailchimp's style guide says you have the same voice all the time but your tone changes; tone adjusts to the moment, including the emotional state of the person addressed.**

No change needed.

- [Mailchimp Content Style Guide](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/voice-and-tone/): “You have the same voice all the time, but your tone changes.”

**In a 2016 Nielsen Norman Group study, 100 participants rated pairs of nearly identical web texts that differed only in tone.**

No change needed.

- [Moran, NN/g (2016)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tone-voice-users/): “In the quantitative survey of 100 American users over the age of 18, we asked the respondents to rate each sample on three 5-point Likert scales.”

**Perceived trustworthiness explained about 52% of the variation in whether people would recommend the organization; friendliness added only about 8% more.**

NN/g's finding that trustworthiness predicts willingness to recommend far more than friendliness fits consumer research showing perceived competence drives purchase intent.

- [Aaker, Vohs and Mogilner, J. Consumer Research (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1086/651566): “consumers are less willing to buy a product made by a nonprofit than a for-profit because of their perception that the firm lacks competence.”

**Warmer tones usually did well in the study, but trust carried far more weight, so warmth pays off only when it doesn't cost credibility.**

Experiments outside UX find informality raises perceived goodwill but can lower perceived competence in professional contexts, which supports the article's caution while NN/g's data show warmth usually helped.

- [The double-edged sword of informality, J. Social and Personal Relationships (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1177/02654075251314810): “informality reduces perceived sender ability in professional contexts, particularly when it violates formal communication expectations.”

### Friendly From a Stranger Reads Differently

**Anaïs Gretry and colleagues ran three experiments comparing brands posting on social media in an informal or a formal style. Informality raised trust when people already knew the brand and lowered it when they didn't. The authors' explanation is social norms: people expect brands to follow them, so casual talk seems appropriate from a familiar brand and inappropriate from an unfamiliar one.**

Later work on chatbots and workplace messages supports the idea that informality works best when it fits the relationship, though one replication did not confirm the 'appropriateness' explanation and a chatbot replication found no familiarity effect.

- [Gretry et al., J. Business Research (2017), via RePEc record (ScienceDirect returned 403)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v74y2017icp77-89.html): “using an informal style can either have a positive or negative effect on brand trust, depending on whether consumers are familiar with the brand or not.”
- [Artificially sincere?, European J. Marketing (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1108/ejm-07-2025-0534): “Informality strengthens brand attitudes and purchase intentions when psychological brand closeness is high (Study 3)”

**Sela and colleagues found that "we" instead of "you and [the brand]" could make people like a real brand more or less depending on whether the implied closeness matched the expected relationship; customers and non-customers reacted differently, and perceived trustworthiness carried the effect.**

A 2025 meta-analysis finds pronoun effects are small and depend on context, consistent with Sela's fit account.

- [Sela, Wheeler and Sarial-Abi, J. Consumer Research (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1086/664972): “these effects depend on whether the closeness implied by the pronoun is consistent with people's expected interactions with the brand.”

**In the NN/g study a playful car-insurance text was rated friendlier than its serious twin but less trustworthy; "the friendliness and irreverence actually undermined users' perceptions of trustworthiness and professionalism."**

No change needed.

- [Moran, NN/g (2016)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tone-voice-users/): “However, in this situation, the friendliness and irreverence actually undermined users’ perceptions of trustworthiness and professionalism.”
- [Humorous response to online customer complaint, European J. Marketing (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1108/ejm-08-2018-0522): “Although humorous responses have an unfavourable influence on perceived trustworthiness of the firm”

**A casual bank text beat its formal version on both friendliness and trustworthiness.**

No change needed.

**Taken together, the research points to a practical test that reaches beyond social posts, though no study has tested it directly: would this sound right coming from a company the reader met yesterday, on the worst day they could be having?**

Service-recovery experiments find formal language from human agents helps most when failures are severe, which supports writing for the reader's worst day.

- [Service recovery agent and language style, Asia Pacific J. Marketing and Logistics (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1108/apjml-03-2026-0722): “Forgiveness is enhanced when robots use informal language and humans use formal language, with the effect mediated by perceived sincerity.”

### A Declined-Card Screen

**Playful register, from a brand she barely knows, about money. Gretry's finding on social media posts suggests this costs trust, and studies of joking replies to customer complaints point the same way.**

Experiments on humorous company replies to complaints found humor lowered perceived trustworthiness, extending Gretry's social-media finding toward failure moments.

- [strategy+business summary of Gretry et al. (2017)](https://strategy-business.com/blog/140-Characters-Can-Make-or-Break-Your-Brand): “informal style — peppered with exclamation points and emojis — increased consumers’ trust when they were familiar with a brand, but had the opposite effect when they lacked previous knowledge of the brand.”
- [Humorous response to online customer complaint, European J. Marketing (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1108/ejm-08-2018-0522): “Although humorous responses have an unfavourable influence on perceived trustworthiness of the firm”
- [Preregistered replication of Glikson et al., Collabra: Psychology (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.90195): “smileys produce a negative effect on perceptions of competence and a positive effect on perceptions of warmth, regardless of the level of formality.”

**A joke that guesses at her finances. Cards fail for many reasons, often dull ones such as an expired date or a bank's fraud check, and a stranger has no business guessing which.**

Payment processors list expired cards and fraud screening among common decline reasons, alongside insufficient funds.

- [Stripe documentation, Declines](https://docs.stripe.com/declines): “Payments can fail for a variety of reasons, including some that help prevent fraudulent transactions.”

### Matching Tone to the Moment

**In bad-news moments (errors, declines, outages), avoid humor and emoji aimed at the person having the problem and write in the most serious tone the voice allows.**

Experiments on company replies to complaints find humor lowers perceived trustworthiness, and humor aimed at the customer does the most damage; gentle humor can work with onlookers.

- [Humorous response to online customer complaint, European J. Marketing (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1108/ejm-08-2018-0522): “aggressive humour further aggravates the negative impact of humorous response on trustworthiness.”
- [Humorous responses to online public complaints, J. Service Research (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1094670521989448): “online observers respond more favorably (in terms of likes, retweets, and purchase intentions) when firms use affiliative humor (e.g., laughing with the complainer) rather than aggressive humor (e.g., laughing at the complainer).”
- [Moran, NN/g (2016)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tone-voice-users/): “If your users are arriving frustrated at an error page, do you really think they want that error message to be a joke?”

**Anniversaries, memories and year-end reviews need a way to opt out, because the moment is not happy for everyone.**

No change needed.

- [Meyer (2014)](https://meyerweb.com/eric/thoughts/2014/12/24/inadvertent-algorithmic-cruelty/): “Where the human aspect fell short, at least with Facebook, was in not providing a way to opt out.”

**Start warm but plain, and let informality grow with familiarity. Save "we" and other closeness cues for when the relationship can carry them.**

Research on pronouns finds that closeness cues like "we" help only when they match the relationship people expect; the effects are small and vary by setting.

- [Gretry et al. (2017), RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v74y2017icp77-89.html): “the use of an informal style is perceived to be appropriate for familiar brands and inappropriate for unfamiliar ones.”
- [Sela et al. (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1086/664972): “these effects depend on whether the closeness implied by the pronoun is consistent with people's expected interactions with the brand.”

### Conclusion

**Tone works as a relationship signal. It tells readers how well a company thinks it knows them, and readers tend to trust companies whose estimate matches their own.**

Studies of pronouns, informal brand posts and informal messages all find that implied closeness helps when it fits the relationship and can backfire when it doesn't.

- [Sela et al. (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1086/664972): “are mediated by perceived brand trustworthiness”
- [The double-edged sword of informality, J. Social and Personal Relationships (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1177/02654075251314810): “informality increases perceived sender benevolence through communicating feelings of closeness”

## What changed in the article

These edits were made on November 12, 2026 after the review.

### Voice Stays Put, Tone Moves: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Warmth helps, but only while it doesn't cost credibility.

**Now reads:** Warmer tones usually did well in the study, but trust carried far more weight, so warmth pays off only when it doesn't cost credibility.

**Why:** The NN/g study mostly found that warmer tones helped: in three of four pairs the friendlier text was rated as trustworthy or more so, and only the playful insurance text lost trust. Other research does show informality can lower perceived competence in professional settings, so the trade-off is real, but the sentence makes warmth sound riskier than the cited study found. Now cites [The double-edged sword of informality, J. Social and Personal Relationships (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1177/02654075251314810).

### Friendly From a Stranger Reads Differently: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Gretry and colleagues found informal brand posts raised trust when people already knew the brand and lowered it when they didn't, because people expect brands to follow social norms, so casual talk seems appropriate only from a familiar brand.

**Now reads:** Anaïs Gretry and colleagues ran three experiments comparing brands posting on social media in an informal or a formal style. Informality raised trust when people already knew the brand and lowered it when they didn't. The authors' explanation is social norms: people expect brands to follow them, so casual talk seems appropriate from a familiar brand and inappropriate from an unfamiliar one.

**Why:** Gretry and colleagues report exactly this pattern and explanation, from three experiments on brand social media posts. The finding has not been directly replicated, and a large replication of a related workplace-email study did not confirm "appropriateness" as the reason, so the mechanism is best presented as the authors' explanation rather than settled fact.

### Friendly From a Stranger Reads Differently: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Taken together, the research suggests a test that applies well beyond social posts: would this sound right coming from a company the reader met yesterday, on the worst day they could be having?

**Now reads:** Taken together, the research points to a practical test that reaches beyond social posts, though no study has tested it directly: would this sound right coming from a company the reader met yesterday, on the worst day they could be having?

**Why:** The test is a sensible synthesis: the cited studies show familiarity matters and NN/g advises considering the reader's emotional state, and service-failure research finds formal language helps human agents more when failures are severe. But no study tested a "worst day" standard, and the Gretry study covered only social media posts, so this is the article's heuristic rather than a research finding.

### A Declined-Card Screen: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** A playful headline ("Oops! Your card got rejected" with an emoji) from a barely known brand, about money, costs trust, as Gretry's finding suggests.

**Now reads:** Playful register, from a brand she barely knows, about money. Gretry's finding on social media posts suggests this costs trust, and studies of joking replies to customer complaints point the same way.

**Why:** Gretry's informal markers were exclamation points and emoji, and the effect on trust reversed for unfamiliar brands, so the link to this headline is reasonable. Their study was about social media posts, not payment errors; studies of joking replies after service failures mostly find a trust cost, though results vary with the kind of humor. Now cites [The bright and dark sides of humorous response to online customer complaint, European J. Marketing (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1108/ejm-08-2018-0522).

### A Declined-Card Screen: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Cards fail for dull reasons, such as an expired date or a bank's fraud check.

**Now reads:** A joke that guesses at her finances. Cards fail for many reasons, often dull ones such as an expired date or a bank's fraud check, and a stranger has no business guessing which.

**Why:** Stripe's documentation confirms that expiration dates and fraud screening are common reasons for declines. It also lists insufficient funds, so not every decline is dull; the article's real point, that the company can't know the reason and shouldn't guess, still holds. Now cites [Stripe documentation, Declines](https://docs.stripe.com/declines).

### Matching Tone to the Moment: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** At first contact, start warm but plain and let informality grow with familiarity; use "you" and the brand name until the relationship is real.

**Now reads:** Start warm but plain, and let informality grow with familiarity. Save "we" and other closeness cues for when the relationship can carry them.

**Why:** Letting informality grow with familiarity follows from Gretry's results. The pronoun advice is a stretch: Sela found that the effect of "we" depends on fit with the relationship, but did not test a rule that "you" plus the brand name is the safe default, and a 2025 meta-analysis finds pronoun effects are small and vary by setting.

### Conclusion: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Tone works as a relationship signal: it tells readers how well a company thinks it knows them, and readers trust the companies whose estimate matches their own.

**Now reads:** Tone works as a relationship signal. It tells readers how well a company thinks it knows them, and readers tend to trust companies whose estimate matches their own.

**Why:** This restates Gretry and Sela as a general rule. Both studies, and newer work on chatbots and informal messages, support the idea that tone works best when its implied closeness fits the relationship. But the evidence comes from specific settings, and one replication did not confirm the appropriateness explanation, so "tend to" is more accurate than a flat law.
