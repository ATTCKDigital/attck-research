# Evidence review: Hedonic vs Utilitarian Motivation

Article: [Hedonic vs Utilitarian Motivation](https://attck.com/blog/hedonic-vs-utilitarian-motivation/)  
Reviewed: November 2026 · Method version 2 · [How these reviews work](https://github.com/ATTCKDigital/attck-research/blob/main/.claude/skills/evidence-review/METHOD.md)

## How this review works

Each factual claim in the article was checked against the source it cites, then challenged by reviewers whose job was to find the strongest evidence against it: replication attempts, meta-analyses, later papers that cite the original work, and research from other fields that study the same mechanism. A separate reviewer weighed both sides, and a script re-checked every quoted source. Where a claim was overstated or misattributed, the article was corrected. The research below supports the article as it now reads.

## Background research

### Introduction

**Strahilevitz and Myers tested a promised charity donation as a purchase incentive for practical necessities (e.g., laundry detergent) and frivolous luxuries (e.g., a hot fudge sundae); across two lab experiments and a field study, the charity incentive did more for the frivolous products.**

No change needed. The meta-analysis is useful context on the size of charity effects in general.

- [Strahilevitz & Myers, Journal of Consumer Research, 1998 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1086/209519): “The results suggest that charity incentives are more effective in promoting frivolous products than in promoting practical products.”

### Two Reasons to Buy the Same Thing

**Consumer research long treated buying as information processing; in 1982 Holbrook and Hirschman argued this missed the experiential side of consumption, directed toward fantasies, feelings and fun.**

No change needed.

- [Holbrook & Hirschman, Journal of Consumer Research, 1982 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1086/208906): “the prevailing information processing model is contrasted with an experiential view that focuses on the symbolic, hedonic, and esthetic nature of consumption.”

**Babin, Darden and Griffin showed that even a shopping trip produces distinct hedonic and utilitarian value, measurable separately.**

No change needed.

- [Babin, Darden & Griffin, Journal of Consumer Research, 1994 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1086/209376): “The results demonstrate that distinct hedonic and utilitarian shopping value dimensions exist and are related to a number of important consumption variables.”

**They are motives as much as product categories. Babin, Darden and Griffin showed that even a shopping trip produces distinct hedonic and utilitarian value, measurable separately. Most products carry some of each, and the moment and the person decide which motive leads.**

Softened 'not product categories' to 'as much as product categories', since the research the article cites does sort goods into the two kinds, and added that most products carry both.

- [Voss, Spangenberg & Grohmann, Journal of Marketing Research, 2003 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.40.3.310.19238): “Results suggest that the hedonic and utilitarian constructs are two distinct dimensions of brand attitude and are reliably and validly measured by the HED/UT scale.”

**Chitturi, Raghunathan and Mahajan found that meeting utilitarian needs produces satisfaction, built on confidence and security, while meeting hedonic wants produces delight, built on cheerfulness and excitement.**

No change needed.

- [Chitturi, Raghunathan & Mahajan, Journal of Marketing, 2008 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.72.3.048): “the primary antecedent feelings of satisfaction are the prevention emotions of confidence and security provided by utilitarian benefits, whereas the primary antecedent feelings of delight are the promotion emotions of cheerfulness and excitement provided by hedonic benefits.”

**Delight did more for word of mouth and repurchase intentions than satisfaction alone.**

No change needed.

### Fun Needs a Reason

**Okada: people want to have fun, and they are more likely to have it when the situation lets them justify it.**

No change needed.

- [Okada, Journal of Marketing Research, 2005 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.42.1.43.56889): “People want to have fun, and they are more likely to have fun if the situation allows them to justify it.”
- [Khan & Dhar, Journal of Marketing Research, 2006 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.43.2.259): “a prior choice, which activates and boosts a positive self-concept, subsequently licenses the choice of a more self-indulgent option.”

**Across four experiments and field studies, preferences reversed with presentation: a hedonic option tended to be rated higher than a comparable utilitarian one when seen alone, but the utilitarian option tended to be chosen when the two appeared side by side.**

No change needed. The 2025 paper is a useful independent confirmation of the pattern.

- [Evaluation mode affects choice of healthy and unhealthy food, Journal of Economic Psychology, 2025 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joep.2025.102832): “Unhealthy food choices decrease when shifting from separate to joint evaluation.”

**People preferred to pay more in time and effort for hedonic goods and more in money for utilitarian ones.**

No change needed.

**Kivetz and Simonson, in studies with about 3,100 consumers, found that higher loyalty-program requirements shifted preferences from necessity rewards toward luxury rewards, because effort reduced the guilt of choosing a luxury.**

No change needed.

- [Kivetz & Simonson, Journal of Marketing Research, 2002 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.39.2.155.19084): “A series of studies with approximately 3100 consumers demonstrated that (1) higher program requirements shift preferences in favor of luxury rewards”

### Pull quote

**Kivetz and Keinan: in the short run, vice is regretted more than virtue, but in the long run virtue is regretted more.**

No change needed. The source note could add the underlying 2006 paper alongside the press release.

- [University of Chicago Press release on Kivetz & Keinan (EurekAlert)](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/482224): “In the short run, vice is regretted more than virtue, but in the long run virtue is regretted more”
- [Kivetz & Keinan, Repenting Hyperopia, Journal of Consumer Research, 2006 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1086/506308): “greater temporal separation between a choice and its assessment enhances the regret (or anticipated regret) of virtuous decisions (e.g., choosing work over pleasure).”

### The comparison table

**Comparison grids are built for utilitarian decisions, and they serve those well. Okada's reversal suggests they can work against the fun option. Put a premium or purely enjoyable item in a grid beside a sensible one and the grid may ask the visitor to defend it. On its own page, with its pleasures described concretely and a reason the visitor can repeat to themselves, it has a better chance to compete on its own terms.**

Hedged the design advice, since Okada tested side-by-side versus single presentation rather than comparison tables, and later work shows the effect depends on which features are easy to judge.

- [Okada, Journal of Marketing Research, 2005 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.42.1.43.56889): “relative preferences between hedonic and utilitarian alternatives can reverse, depending on how the immediate purchase situation presents itself.”
- [Evaluation mode affects choice of healthy and unhealthy food, Journal of Economic Psychology, 2025 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joep.2025.102832): “Unhealthy food choices decrease when shifting from separate to joint evaluation.”

Narrowed 'feel-good bonuses' to the donation the study tested, and added sales-promotion research on matching the offer to the product.

Kept points as the example the research supports and flagged streaks and challenges as untested extensions.

### The downgrade screen

**Dhar and Wertenbroch found that hedonic qualities weigh more when people decide what to give up than when they decide what to acquire.**

No change needed.

- [Dhar & Wertenbroch, Journal of Marketing Research, 2000 (OpenAlex abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.37.1.60.18718): “the relative salience of hedonic dimensions is greater when consumers decide which of several items to give up (forfeiture choices) than when they decide which item to acquire (acquisition choices).”

**In a field survey, owners of relatively hedonic cars valued them more, relative to market prices, than owners of relatively utilitarian ones.**

No change needed.

**A downgrade or cancellation flow is a giving-up decision, and the enjoyable features are likely to weigh more there than they did at sign-up. If it strips them first, expect more resistance than the pricing table suggests, and say plainly what the person keeps.**

Softened the prediction about downgrade flows, which extends a lab finding on giving up items to a setting that has not been tested.

Changed 'consumer psychologists' to 'behavioral scientists'. Principle names should be checked against a copy of the book.

Turned 'prefer' into 'try before', since no study ranks these incentives against discounts directly.

## What changed in the article

These edits were made on November 12, 2026 after the review.

### Two Reasons to Buy the Same Thing: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Hedonic and utilitarian are motives, not product categories; the moment and the person decide which motive leads for the same product.

**Now reads:** They are motives as much as product categories. Babin, Darden and Griffin showed that even a shopping trip produces distinct hedonic and utilitarian value, measurable separately. Most products carry some of each, and the moment and the person decide which motive leads.

**Why:** Research does show that one product or activity carries both hedonic and utilitarian value, and that the mix differs by brand and person. But 'not product categories' goes too far: most of the studies the article itself cites classify goods as hedonic or utilitarian, and a standard scale measures these dimensions for product categories. Now cites [Voss, Spangenberg & Grohmann, Measuring the Hedonic and Utilitarian Dimensions of Consumer Attitude, Journal of Marketing Research (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.40.3.310.19238).

### Fun Needs a Reason: contested

**Claim reviewed:** Both patterns come from the need for justification: a side-by-side comparison makes the indulgent choice harder to defend, while effort feels like earning it.

**Now reads:** Okada traces both patterns to the same place. A side-by-side comparison makes the indulgent choice harder to defend, while effort feels like earning it. The effort half has held up in later experiments. The comparison half is less settled: decision researchers have long explained side-by-side reversals by how easy each feature is to judge, and practical benefits are usually easier to judge next to an alternative, so the comparison favors them with or without any guilt.

**Why:** Okada does say both patterns come from the need to justify, and the effort half has held up: later experiments found people will pay more in effort for treats and more in money for practical goods, with the effect strongest among guilt-prone people. The comparison half is still argued over. Two well-established accounts from decision research explain side-by-side reversals by which features are easy to judge or compare, with no guilt involved, and the one set of studies built to tell the accounts apart found for the easy-to-judge account and showed the effect flips when the practical feature is easy to judge. Now cites [Hsee, Loewenstein, Blount & Bazerman, Preference Reversals Between Joint and Separate Evaluations of Options: A Review and Theoretical Analysis, Psychological Bulletin (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.5.576).

### The comparison table: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Comparison grids work against the fun option: a premium or enjoyable item placed in a grid beside a sensible one must be defended, while on its own page, with concrete pleasures and a reason, it competes on its own terms.

**Now reads:** Comparison grids are built for utilitarian decisions, and they serve those well. Okada's reversal suggests they can work against the fun option. Put a premium or purely enjoyable item in a grid beside a sensible one and the grid may ask the visitor to defend it. On its own page, with its pleasures described concretely and a reason the visitor can repeat to themselves, it has a better chance to compete on its own terms.

**Why:** Okada's finding fits the advice in direction, and the 2025 food studies found the same drop for indulgent options in side-by-side choice. But neither tested comparison tables or product pages, and the 2025 work shows the effect can flip when the practical benefit is easy to judge on its own. 'Competes on its own terms' is the article's inference, not a finding.

### The offer and the reward: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Feel-good bonuses, such as a donation, suit indulgences better than necessities.

**Now reads:** Match the incentive to the motive. A promised donation has done more for indulgences than for necessities, and promotions in general work best when what they offer fits what the product is for.

**Why:** The charity study supports this for donations specifically. 'Feel-good bonuses' as a class goes further than one study, though sales-promotion research supports the broader idea that a promotion works best when its benefit matches the product. A meta-analysis also finds charity tie-ins have only a weak average effect on behavior. Now cites [Chandon, Wansink & Laurent, A Benefit Congruency Framework of Sales Promotion Effectiveness, Journal of Marketing (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.64.4.65.18071).

### The offer and the reward: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Rewards earned through effort, such as streaks, challenges and points, suit luxuries, because the effort supplies the justification.

**Now reads:** Rewards earned through effort, such as loyalty points, tilt people toward luxuries, because the effort helps justify the treat. Streaks and challenges may work the same way, though they have not been tested as directly.

**Why:** Kivetz and Simonson tested frequency programs, the buy-and-earn kind, so points are a fair example. Streaks and challenges were not tested, and no gamification study was found on this. A 2025 study also found that a sense of achievement, more than felt effort, makes people feel they have earned a reward.

### The offer and the reward: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** For a purchase made to get a job done, a plain saving or a faster route to finished is usually the stronger offer.

**Now reads:** For a purchase made to get a job done, a plain saving is usually the stronger offer, since price promotions have worked better for practical products than for indulgent ones. A faster route to finished likely helps for the same reason.

**Why:** No source is cited, but sales-promotion experiments found monetary promotions work better for utilitarian products than for hedonic ones, and Okada found people prefer to pay in money for utilitarian goods. The 'faster route to finished' half has no direct evidence. Now cites [Chandon, Wansink & Laurent, A Benefit Congruency Framework of Sales Promotion Effectiveness, Journal of Marketing (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.64.4.65.18071).

### The downgrade screen: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** A downgrade or cancellation flow is a giving-up decision; if it strips the enjoyable features first, expect it to hurt more than the pricing table predicted.

**Now reads:** A downgrade or cancellation flow is a giving-up decision, and the enjoyable features are likely to weigh more there than they did at sign-up. If it strips them first, expect more resistance than the pricing table suggests, and say plainly what the person keeps.

**Why:** Dhar and Wertenbroch studied choosing which item to give up, so treating a downgrade as a giving-up decision is a reasonable step. But no study tests downgrade or cancellation flows, and 'expect it to hurt more than the pricing table predicted' is stated with more confidence than the evidence allows.

### Happy Money: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Dunn and Norton, two consumer psychologists, boil the research on spending and happiness down to five principles, including 'Buy Experiences', 'Make It a Treat' and 'Invest in Others' (Simon & Schuster, 2013, 197 pages).

**Now reads:** Two behavioral scientists boil the research on spending and happiness down to five principles. “Buy Experiences,” “Make It a Treat” and “Invest in Others” line up with the hedonic motive, the justification finding and the charity incentive covered here.

**Why:** The book record confirms five principles running from experiences to spending on others, 197 pages, Simon & Schuster 2013. Two details are loose: Dunn is a social psychologist and Norton a business-school behavioral scientist, so 'consumer psychologists' is not quite right, and the 'Make It a Treat' and 'Invest in Others' names were not confirmed from an accessible page. A large registered replication also found the giving-to-others effect real but smaller and less consistent than early studies.

### Do's and Don'ts: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** For enjoyment purchases, prefer effort-based rewards and feel-good bonuses to small discounts.

**Now reads:** Try effort-based rewards and feel-good bonuses before small discounts, which tend to do more for practical purchases.

**Why:** Neither cited study compares effort rewards or donations against small discounts. Sales-promotion research gives indirect support, since price cuts work better for practical products than for indulgent ones, but 'prefer to small discounts' is a ranking no study here tested. Now cites [Chandon, Wansink & Laurent, A Benefit Congruency Framework of Sales Promotion Effectiveness, Journal of Marketing (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.64.4.65.18071).
