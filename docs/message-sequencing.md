# Evidence review: Message Sequencing

Article: [Message Sequencing](https://attck.com/blog/message-sequencing/)  
Reviewed: November 2026 · Method version 2 · [How these reviews work](https://github.com/ATTCKDigital/attck-research/blob/main/.claude/skills/evidence-review/METHOD.md)

## How this review works

Each factual claim in the article was checked against the source it cites, then challenged by reviewers whose job was to find the strongest evidence against it: replication attempts, meta-analyses, later papers that cite the original work, and research from other fields that study the same mechanism. A separate reviewer weighed both sides, and a script re-checked every quoted source. Where a claim was overstated or misattributed, the article was corrected. The research below supports the article as it now reads.

## Background research

### What a Sequence Decides

**In 1966, Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser found that people who had agreed to a small request were more likely to agree to a larger one later, even when a different person made the second request. Later reviews found the effect is real but small and does not always appear.**

Meta-analyses (Beaman et al. 1983; Dillard, Hunter & Burgoon 1984) found the foot-in-the-door effect replicable but small, and a 2019 direct replication of the original procedure found no effect in 2013.

- [Freedman & Fraser (1966)](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0023552): “2 experiments were conducted to test the proposition that once someone has agreed to a small request he is more likely to comply with a larger request.”

**The foot-in-the-door effect held even when a different person made the second request.**

The second experiment used different requesters; the usual self-perception explanation for this transfer has mixed support.

**For sequencing, the lesson is modest but useful: an early, easy yes can make the next ask easier, so the first message should ask for something small that genuinely helps.**

The foot-in-the-door effect is small and depends on conditions; a 2025 field study found that ordering sign-up questions from least to most intrusive raised disclosure online.

- [International Journal of Electronic Commerce, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/10864415.2025.2594327): “Results from both studies confirm that the proposed mechanisms significantly increase users’ disclosure behavior during sign-up.”

### Pre-Suasion

**Cialdini's follow-up to Influence argues that what a person is focused on just before a request shapes how the request lands.**

Pre-Suasion is about directing attention in the moment before a message; priming research supports a small effect of what people have just seen.

- [Mandel & Johnson, When Web Pages Influence Choice: Effects of Visual Primes on Experts and Novices, Journal of Consumer Research, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1086/341573): “Our on-line experiments manipulate the background pictures and colors of a Web page, affecting consumer product choice.”
- [Weingarten et al., From primed concepts to action: A meta-analysis of the behavioral effects of incidentally presented words, Psychological Bulletin, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000030): “Findings revealed a small behavioral priming effect (dFE = 0.332, dRE = 0.352), which was robust across methodological procedures”

**It makes a well-referenced case for treating what comes just before a message as a design decision, and it revisits consistency, the principle behind the foot-in-the-door effect, as one of the ideas to bring to mind before an ask.**

Pre-Suasion's thesis is attention in the moment before a request. Its chapter 10 revisits consistency among the six Influence principles as something to focus attention on before an ask, and chapter 14 covers making change last; the fuller treatment of commitment and consistency is in Influence. Burger (1999) reviews consistency and commitment as foot-in-the-door processes.

- [Pre-Suasion, chapter 2 summary in the author's introduction, sample text hosted by the Library of Congress (LCCN 2016298117)](https://www.loc.gov/catdir/enhancements/fy1702/2016298117-s.html): “the factor most likely to determine a person's choice in a situation is often not the one that offers the most accurate or useful counsel; instead, it is the one that has been elevated in attention”
- [Library of Congress catalog record for ISBN 9781501109799 (MODS via LC SRU gateway)](http://lx2.loc.gov:210/LCDB?version=1.1&operation=searchRetrieve&query=bath.isbn%3D9781501109799&maximumRecords=2&recordSchema=mods): “Includes bibliographical references (pages 235-326) and index.”
- [Bolkan & Andersen, Image Induction and Social Influence: Explication and Initial Tests, Basic and Applied Social Psychology, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1080/01973530903316898): “By preceding a request with a question that creates a salient self-image in a target, targets are more likely to behave consistently with that image by complying with the request.”
- [Burger, The Foot-in-the-Door Compliance Procedure: A Multiple-Process Analysis and Review, Personality and Social Psychology Review, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327957pspr0304_2): “Several psychological processes that may be set in motion with a FITD manipulation are identified: self-perception, psychological reactance, conformity, consistency, attributions, and commitment.”

### Does Repetition Persuade or Wear People Out?

**In 1979, Cacioppo and Petty played people the same persuasive message one, three or five times in a row; agreement first rose, then fell as the repetitions continued.**

The rise-then-fall pattern also appears in a 2017 meta-analysis of mere exposure, but not in every study (Rethans et al. 1986), and brand attachment slows wear-out.

- [Cacioppo & Petty (1979)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.37.1.97): “In both experiments, agreement first increased, then decreased as exposure frequency increased (regardless of the position advocated)”
- [Montoya, Horton, Vevea, Citkowicz & Lauber, A re-examination of the mere exposure effect, Psychological Bulletin, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000085): “the mere exposure effect was characterized by a positive slope and negative quadratic effect consistent with an inverted-U shaped curve.”

**Counterarguments dropped, then climbed again, while thoughts about unrelated things rose as the plays added up.**

A 2023 preregistered replication links fatigue from repeated messages to more counterarguing.

- [Human Communication Research, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/hcr/hqad021): “it is positively correlated with counterargument and negatively correlated with attention and message elaboration.”

**A 2015 meta-analysis by Schmidt and Eisend, pooling experimental studies of advertising repetition, found that attitudes reached their maximum at about ten exposures, while recall kept climbing in a straight line through at least the eighth.**

Belief in repeated statements can keep rising past 20 exposures (Hassan & Barber 2021), so the ten-exposure peak applies to ad attitudes in experiments.

- [Schmidt & Eisend (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1080/00913367.2015.1018460): “in an experimental setting maximum attitude is reached at approximately ten exposures, while recall increases linearly and does not level off before the eighth exposure.”

**Spaced exposures strengthened the effect on attitudes toward the brand, massed exposures helped recall, and every repetition effect faded with time.**

Schmidt and Eisend found spacing helps attitudes while massing helps recall; spacing research in memory (Cepeda et al. 2006) supports longer gaps for longer-lasting effects.

- [Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer, Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks, Psychological Bulletin, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354): “the ISI producing maximal retention increased as retention interval increased.”

**One likely reason the two findings differ: five plays back to back is massed repetition, the kind that invites counterarguing, and spreading the same exposures out seems to raise the ceiling.**

The spacing half of this synthesis is supported by Janiszewski, Noel & Sawyer (2003).

- [Janiszewski, Noel & Sawyer, Journal of Consumer Research, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1086/374692): “The effects of repeated advertising exposures depend on the size of the interval, or space, between ad exposures.”

**In a large randomized test of retargeted ads for an online home-improvement retailer, Sahni and colleagues found that retargeting brought 14.6% more users back within four weeks.**

Retargeting effects depend on ad content: generic ads beat personalized ones for a travel firm (Lambrecht & Tucker 2013).

- [Sahni, Narayanan & Kalyanam (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022243718813987): “They find that switching on experimental retargeting causes 14.6% more users to return to the website within four weeks.”

### Does Repetition Persuade or Wear People Out? / Days 1 to 3

**In that study, early ads carried the most weight, and later ones leaned on them.**

Free-trial research links inactivity late in a trial to lower conversion, which supports front-loading useful help.

- [Sahni, Narayanan & Kalyanam (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022243718813987): “The impact of retargeting decreases as the time since the consumer first visited the website increases”
- [Yoganarasimhan, Barzegary & Pani, Management Science, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2022.4507): “show that long stretches of inactivity at the end of the trial are associated with lower conversions.”
- [Klasnja et al., Annals of Behavioral Medicine, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/abm/kay067): “Providing any type of suggestion versus no suggestion initially increased the step count by 66% (167 steps; p < .01), but this effect diminished over time.”

### Days 1 to 3

**BJ Fogg: "Behavior happens when Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt come together at the same time. When a behavior does not occur, at least one of those three elements is missing."**

Searches found the model used as a design framework in intervention studies, not tested directly.

- [Fogg Behavior Model, behaviormodel.org](https://behaviormodel.org/): “Behavior happens when Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt come together at the same time. When a behavior does not occur, at least one of those three elements is missing.”

### Days 4 to 11

**The core claim repeats while the example changes, so for someone engaged enough to read, each exposure gives her something new to think about instead of something to argue with.**

Varying the substance of repeated ads works best when people are motivated to process them (Schumann, Petty & Clemons 1990).

- [Schumann, Petty & Clemons, Journal of Consumer Research (1990)](https://doi.org/10.1086/208549): “Experiment 2 found that a substantive variation strategy (variation in relevant product attributes across multiple presentations) was more influential when motivation to process the ad was high.”
- [Haugtvedt, Schumann, Schneier & Warren, Advertising Repetition and Variation Strategies: Implications for Understanding Attitude Strength, Journal of Consumer Research, 1994](https://doi.org/10.1086/209391): “type of advertising repetition strategy differentially influences the extent to which individuals resist change in the face of a counterpersuasive attack.”

## What changed in the article

These edits were made on November 12, 2026 after the review.

### What a Sequence Decides: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** In 1966, Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser found that people who had agreed to a small request were more likely to agree to a larger one later.

**Now reads:** In 1966, Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser found that people who had agreed to a small request were more likely to agree to a larger one later, even when a different person made the second request. Later reviews found the effect is real but small and does not always appear.

**Why:** Freedman and Fraser did report this result. Later meta-analyses found the effect is real but small, nearly half of studies found nothing or the reverse, and a 2019 direct replication found no effect in 2013 data. The article presents it as a solid finding without saying how fragile it is. Now cites [Beaman, Cole, Preston, Klentz & Steblay, Fifteen Years of Foot-in-the-Door Research: A Meta-Analysis, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin (1983)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167283092002).

### What a Sequence Decides: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** An early, easy yes changes how the next ask is received, so the first message should ask for something small that genuinely helps.

**Now reads:** For sequencing, the lesson is modest but useful: an early, easy yes can make the next ask easier, so the first message should ask for something small that genuinely helps.

**Why:** Starting with a small ask is a reasonable lesson, but the article calls it plain. Meta-analyses find the effect small, and one found it needed a prosocial request and no incentive attached to the first ask, which a commercial onboarding email may not meet. The 1966 study involved strangers at the door, not product messages. Now cites [Burger, The Foot-in-the-Door Compliance Procedure: A Multiple-Process Analysis and Review, Personality and Social Psychology Review (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327957pspr0304_2).

### Pre-Suasion: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Cialdini's Pre-Suasion argues that what a person has just seen or been asked shapes how the next request lands.

**Now reads:** Cialdini's follow-up to Influence argues that what a person is focused on just before a request shapes how the request lands.

**Why:** Pre-Suasion's central argument is that what people focus on just before a request shapes their response, so 'what a person has just seen' is fair. 'Or been asked' leans on foot-in-the-door material that belongs more to Cialdini's earlier book, Influence. The book description quote could not be re-fetched, so this rests on the reviewer's reading and related research.

### Pre-Suasion: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Pre-Suasion is the research case for treating message order as a design decision, and its material on commitment and consistency extends the foot-in-the-door finding.

**Now reads:** It makes a well-referenced case for treating what comes just before a message as a design decision, and it revisits consistency, the principle behind the foot-in-the-door effect, as one of the ideas to bring to mind before an ask.

**Why:** Cialdini's own chapter overview, hosted by the Library of Congress as the book's sample text, confirms that the book's thesis is what a person attends to just before a request, that it revisits consistency, one of the six principles from Influence, as a concept to bring to mind before an ask, and that it closes with a chapter on making changes last. With about 90 pages of references it is a research-based case, so the article's description is broadly right. But the fuller treatment of commitment and consistency is in Influence, and nothing found shows Pre-Suasion extending the foot-in-the-door research itself, so 'extends the foot-in-the-door finding' overstates what the book does. Now cites [Burger, The Foot-in-the-Door Compliance Procedure: A Multiple-Process Analysis and Review, Personality and Social Psychology Review (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327957pspr0304_2).

### Pre-Suasion: not supported

**Claim reviewed:** Pre-Suasion, Robert Cialdini, Simon & Schuster, 2016, 432 pages.

**Now reads:** Robert Cialdini | Simon & Schuster | 2016413 Pages

**Why:** The Library of Congress record for the cited ISBN (9781501109799) gives the extent as xiii, 413 pages, and Google Books lists the same September 2016 Simon & Schuster hardcover at 413 pages. The 432 figure belongs to the June 2018 Simon & Schuster paperback (ISBN 9781501109805). The card pairs the 2016 edition with the paperback's page count, so the number is wrong for the edition it cites. Now cites [Library of Congress catalog record, Pre-suasion: a revolutionary way to influence and persuade, LCCN 2016298117](https://lccn.loc.gov/2016298117).

### Does Repetition Persuade or Wear People Out?: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Counterarguments dropped, then climbed again, while thoughts about unrelated things increased with every play.

**Now reads:** Counterarguments dropped, then climbed again, while thoughts about unrelated things rose as the plays added up.

**Why:** The paper reports that counterarguments fell then rose, and off-topic thoughts rose, as exposures went from one to three to five. 'With every play' slightly overstates a trend measured at three levels, but the direction is right.

### Does Repetition Persuade or Wear People Out?: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Spaced exposures strengthened the effect of repetition on attitudes toward the brand, and every repetition effect faded with time.

**Now reads:** Spaced exposures strengthened the effect on attitudes toward the brand, massed exposures helped recall, and every repetition effect faded with time.

**Why:** The meta-analysis does find spaced exposures help attitudes and that effects fade over time. It also found massed exposures help recall, which the article leaves out, so the reader hears only the half that favors spacing.

### Does Repetition Persuade or Wear People Out?: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Five plays back to back is massed repetition, the kind that invites counterarguing; spread the same exposures out and the ceiling rises.

**Now reads:** One likely reason the two findings differ: five plays back to back is massed repetition, the kind that invites counterarguing, and spreading the same exposures out seems to raise the ceiling.

**Why:** This is the article's own way of reconciling two studies, and it fits the spacing research. Neither cited source tests whether spacing reduces counterarguing, and one TV-ad study found no ceiling at all, so it reads better as an interpretation than a finding. Now cites [Janiszewski, Noel & Sawyer, A Meta-analysis of the Spacing Effect in Verbal Learning, Journal of Consumer Research (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1086/374692).

### Does Repetition Persuade or Wear People Out? / Days 1 to 3: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Early messages carry the most weight and later ones lean on them; the retargeting result suggests the first days of a trial carry the most weight, so the best help belongs there.

**Now reads:** In that study, early ads carried the most weight, and later ones leaned on them.

**Why:** The retargeting study measured banner ads shown to people who left a store's site, not help sent to someone already in a trial, so 'early messages carry the most weight' is stated more broadly than the study allows. Trial and health-prompt studies point the same way, but one ad study found what works early differs from what works later. Now cites [Yoganarasimhan, Barzegary & Pani, Design and Evaluation of Optimal Free Trials, Management Science (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2022.4507).

### Days 4 to 11: supported with caveats

**Claim reviewed:** Repeating the core claim while changing the example gives each exposure something new to think about instead of something to argue with.

**Now reads:** The core claim repeats while the example changes, so for someone engaged enough to read, each exposure gives her something new to think about instead of something to argue with.

**Why:** Advertising research supports varying the content of repeated messages, but mainly when people are motivated to think about them; when they are not, surface changes matter more. The idea is uncited, and the likely source is Schumann, Petty and Clemons (1990). A trial user exploring her own project is probably motivated, so the advice fits. Now cites [Schumann, Petty & Clemons, Predicting the Effectiveness of Different Strategies of Advertising Variation, Journal of Consumer Research (1990)](https://doi.org/10.1086/208549).

### Days 12 to 14: contested

**Claim reviewed:** A summary of what she built does more than a last-minute discount, which mainly tells her the price was negotiable.

**Now reads:** A summary of what she built and what stays available after upgrading reminds her of value she has already seen. A last-minute discount can still prompt action before the deadline, but online limited-time offers often read as a sales tactic, and over time discounts make customers more price-sensitive.

**Why:** Still no study compares an end-of-trial usage summary with a last-minute discount. The summary half has indirect support: a 2025 meta-analysis finds people value what they built themselves, free-trial customers respond to usage and to marketing communication, and inactivity late in a trial predicts lower conversion. The discount half cuts both ways: online limited-time offers often fail to show the effects seen offline and trigger skepticism, and discounts make customers more price-sensitive over time, but deadlines do accelerate purchases and, in field experiments, deeper discounts raised future purchases by first-time customers. The article states a mixed picture as settled, and 'mainly tells her the price was negotiable' names one effect among several. Now cites [Examining the Efficacy of Time Scarcity Marketing Promotions in Online Retail, Journal of Marketing Research (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1177/00222437221118856).
